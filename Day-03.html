Question 1Practice exam
Which player has a dominant strategy in the following game?
Player 2
LeftRight
Player 1  Up2,  −13,  −2
Player 1  Down9,  33.1,  −3

Each cell is (player 1's payoff, player 2's payoff).

c. Both players.

Player 1: compare his two rows column by column. Against Left, Down gives 9 and Up gives 2. Against Right, Down gives 3.1 and Up gives 3. Down wins in both columns, so Down is strictly dominant.

Player 2: compare her two columns row by row. In row Up, Left gives −1 and Right gives −2. In row Down, Left gives 3 and Right gives −3. Left wins in both rows, so Left is strictly dominant.

Why 3.1 is in the question. It is there to catch a fast eye. If Down's second payoff had been 2.9 instead of 3.1, Down would beat Up in one column and lose in the other, and player 1 would have no dominant strategy. One tenth of a point flips the whole answer, so check both columns, every time.
Question 2Practice exam
How many Nash equilibria (pure or mixed) exist in the following game?
Player 2
LeftRight
Player 1  Up5,  −16,  −2
Player 1  Down4,  37,  −3

Each cell is (player 1's payoff, player 2's payoff).

b. 1: the single profile (Up, Left).

Start with player 2, because she is the one with the easy decision. In row Up her choice is −1 versus −2, so Left. In row Down it is 3 versus −3, so Left again. Left is strictly dominant for player 2, so she plays it no matter what.

Player 1 knows this, so he only ever faces the Left column: Up gives 5, Down gives 4. He plays Up. (Up, Left) is the unique Nash equilibrium.

Why there is no mixed one hiding. Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies never destroys an equilibrium, and here it leaves exactly one cell standing. When one cell survives, that cell is the only equilibrium there is, pure or mixed. So the count is 1, not 2 or 3.

Careful with player 1. He has no dominant strategy of his own: Down beats Up against Right (7 vs 6) but loses against Left (4 vs 5). That does not matter, because Right never gets played.
Question 3Practice examtwo correct answers
Assume that 10 players play the 2/3-average competition: each player chooses an integer between 1 and 100, and the player whose number is closest to 2/3 of the average of the ten numbers wins. What would be a best reply against opponents who play 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 40, 50, 50, 89?
Both b (22) and c (23) are correct. Either one is marked right here.

The professor says so himself in the solutions: "Both 22 and 23 will win against these 9 numbers, so, accidentally, both are best replies (I overlooked the fact that there are two best replies when writing the question)." So if you chose the other one, you were not wrong.

The method. The nine opponents sum to 334. Add your own bid, divide by 10, take two thirds. Then see whose number is closest to that target.
• bid 1 → sum 335, average 33.5, target 22.33. You are 21.3 away; the opponent playing 20 is only 2.3 away. You lose.
• bid 22 → sum 356, average 35.6, target 23.73. You are 1.7 away; the nearest opponent (20) is 3.7 away. You win.
• bid 23 → sum 357, average 35.7, target 23.80. You are 0.8 away; the nearest opponent (20) is 3.8 away. You win.
• bid 33 → sum 367, average 36.7, target 24.47. You are 8.5 away; 20 is 4.5 away. You lose.

The trap that catches everyone: option (a). 1 is the Nash equilibrium of this game, the answer when everyone reasons all the way down. It is not the best reply to these nine actual numbers. A best reply answers the opponents you were given, not the opponents theory says you should have.

Rule of thumb. Your own bid drags the average up, so the target sits a little above two thirds of the opponents' average. Here the opponents average 37.1; two thirds of that is 24.7, and the winning band turns out to be 21–28.
Question 4Practice exam
Which of the following games is a sequential game with perfect information: (I) chess, (II) poker, (III) prisoner's dilemma?
a. Only chess.

"Sequential with perfect information" asks two things at once: do players move one after another, and does each player know everything that has already happened?

• Chess: yes to both. The whole board is visible, and nothing is hidden.
• Poker: sequential, but imperfect information. You cannot see your opponent's cards. Turn-taking alone is not enough.
• Prisoner's dilemma: not sequential at all. The two choices are made at the same time.

The trap is (d). Poker feels like the classic strategy game, and it does have turns, so it is easy to wave it through. The hidden cards are exactly what disqualifies it.
Question 5rebuilt question
What will be the payoff of John (player 1) in the backward-induction equilibrium of the following sequential game with Mary (player 2)?
John moves, then Mary, then John again.

Rebuilt question. On the original paper this tree is a picture with no readable text layer, so its payoffs could not be recovered. And unlike the matrices, no arithmetic in the solution pins them down. This tree was built to the same structure the professor's solution describes ("John plays Down then Go, and Mary plays Left"), with fresh numbers, and re-solved to check. It is not the professor's tree.

d. 5. Solve from the last node backwards. Never from the front.

Step 1: John's second node (the far right). Go gives him 5, Stop gives him 3. He takes Go. So that whole branch is worth (5, 4).
Step 2: Mary's node. She now compares Left, which leads to the 4 she gets at Go, against Right, which gives her 1. She takes Left.
Step 3: John's first node. Up gives him 2. Down now leads, through Mary's Left and his own Go, to 5. He takes Down.

Equilibrium: John plays (Down, Go), Mary plays Left. John's payoff is 5.

Every wrong option is a real number on the tree. That is deliberate, and it is the standard trap in tree questions. 4 is Mary's payoff in the very cell you land on. It is the answer you give if you read the wrong half of the pair. 6 is the biggest number in sight, but John only reaches it if Mary hands it to him, and she never will: Right pays her 1. 2 and 3 are branches that get pruned.

Ask yourself twice: which player is the question asking about, and is that number reachable in equilibrium?
Question 6tree redrawn
Which game is described by the following game tree?

Tree redrawn. The original picture could not be read, so this tree was drawn from the professor's own written description of it: "Player 1 offers to give the opponent part of an endowment of 10, and player 2 (the responder) decides whether to accept the offer, or to reject it (in which case, both players get 0)." The shape, and therefore the answer, is his.

c. Ultimatum.

Read the tree as a story: one player proposes a split of a fixed sum, the other says yes or no, and if he says no they both get nothing. That "both get zero" branch is the signature of the ultimatum game: the responder's only power is to burn the pie.

How to rule the others out, fast:
• Dictator: same proposal, but the responder gets no move at all. Here he has two branches, so it is not dictator.
• Prisoner's dilemma: both choose at the same time, and it is drawn as a 2×2 matrix, not a tree.
• Centipede: a long chain of pass-or-take nodes, turn by turn, with the pot growing at every step. Two moves is far too short.
• Stag hunt: again a 2×2 game where both choose at the same time, not a tree.

Worth remembering for the behavioural questions: backward induction says offer the smallest crumb and it will be accepted. Real people do not: the modal offer is 50%, the median about 40%, and offers below 20% are often rejected out of anger.
Question 7Practice exammatrix rebuilt
Which type of game is described by the following payoff matrix?
Player 2
ab
Player 1  a4,  40,  3
Player 1  b3,  03,  3

Each cell is (player 1's payoff, player 2's payoff).

Matrix rebuilt from the professor's solution, which states every number in words: two pure equilibria, (a,a) "yielding 4 to each player but bearing the risk of giving 0 if the opponent plays b", and (b,b) "which gives each player 3, and is safe in the sense that the player gets 3 regardless of the opponent's play". Re-solved to confirm it gives his answer.

b. Stag hunt.

Find the equilibria first. Underline player 1's best reply in each column: in column a, 4 beats 3, so a. In column b, 3 beats 0, so b. Player 2 is symmetric. Both underlined in (a,a) and in (b,b): two pure equilibria.

Now tell them apart by who prefers which. That is the whole classification:
• Stag hunt: two equilibria, and both players agree which is better. (a,a) at 4 each beats (b,b) at 3 each. One is the risky rich one, the other is safe. This game.
• Battle of the sexes / chicken: two equilibria, but each player prefers a different one. Here they want the same one, so both are out.
• Prisoner's dilemma: needs a dominant strategy for each player. Neither player has one here (a is better against a, b is better against b), so it cannot be a PD.
• Dictator: not a matrix game at all.

The tension the game is built on. Row b pays 3 whether the opponent plays a or b. It is completely safe. Row a pays 4 if he joins you and 0 if he doesn't. Rousseau's version: hunt the stag together and eat well, or grab a rabbit alone and eat something. The safe equilibrium is called risk-dominant; the rich one is Pareto-dominant.
Question 8Practice exam
Which percentage of people, approximately, choose to cooperate (split) in the high-stake TV prisoner's dilemma game (split or steal) in the show Golden Balls?
c. 50%. Straight recall: "individual players cooperate about 50% of the time".

The point behind the number. Theory says defect: stealing is dominant, and here it is worth tens of thousands of dollars. Half the contestants split anyway. Raising the stakes barely moves it. So the gap from theory is not just something that happens when the money is small.

Keep these four apart. The exam mixes them:
• Golden Balls cooperation: about 50%
• Lab prisoner's dilemma, first round: about 45%, falling roughly 2 points per round
• Dictator game, median gift: about 25%
• Players choosing the 2/3-contest equilibrium of 1: about 10%

That 10% is why option (a) is sitting there: it is a real number from the course, just from a different experiment.

A player plays a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma that ends after 10 rounds. The payoff matrix in each round is:
Opponent cooperatesOpponent defects
You cooperate1,  1−1,  2
You defect2,  −10,  0

Each cell is (your payoff, opponent's payoff).

Matrix rebuilt, and one word of the stem repaired. Three of the four cells are fixed by the arithmetic printed in the professor's solution. He works the winning payoff out as 1×5 + 2×5 = 15 (so mutual cooperation pays 1 and a lone defection pays 2) and the deviation as "at most 2 + 0" (so mutual defection pays 0). The fourth cell, the sucker payoff, is not recovered. It is chosen, and set to −1. Be clear about what that can affect: it changes nothing that is marked, because it never appears on the winning line or in any comparison that decides the answer. It touches exactly one number on this page: the total of 5 quoted below for option (e), which is a wrong answer either way. And the stem: the paper says the opponent "imitates the opponent's most recent action", which cannot be what he means. His own solution reasons about your action being copied back at you. It reads "the player's" here. That is a repair of an obvious slip, not his wording.

The player wishes to maximise her sum of payoffs. What will be the player's best reply against an opponent who cooperates in odd rounds, and imitates the player's most recent action (from the previous round) in even rounds?
a. Cooperate in odd rounds, defect in even rounds, worth 15.

First, read the opponent's rule slowly. In odd rounds (1, 3, 5, 7, 9) he cooperates whatever you did, so those are free. In even rounds (2, 4, 6, 8, 10) he copies whatever you played last round. So your odd-round move buys or costs you the even round that follows it.

That makes each odd/even pair a small game of its own. Cooperate in the odd round: you get 1, he cooperates next round, and you defect on him for 2. Total 3 per pair, five pairs, 15. Defect in the odd round instead: you get 2, but he copies you and defects next round, so the best you can salvage is 0. Total 2 per pair, which is worse.

Score the other options:
• b. Defect always → 2, 0, 2, 0, … = 10
• c. Cooperate except the last round → nine 1s plus a final 2 = 11
• d. Cooperate always → ten 1s = 10
• e. Defect odd, cooperate even → 2 then −1, five times = 5, the worst of the lot. You defect exactly when it is free for him to punish you, and cooperate exactly when he is punishing.

The lesson this question is really testing. Cooperating is not being nice here; it is buying a cheap defection next round. Against a rule-following opponent, look for the moves he cannot punish. Note also that the famous answer, "play tit-for-tat", is not even on the list. When it is offered on a real paper it is almost never right.

Cournot competition

Two firms choose a quantity at the same time, each from {30, 35, 40, 50, 60}. Marginal cost is 20 and the price is P = 140 − Q1 − Q2, so each firm's profit is (120 − Q1 − Q2) × its own quantity. Rows are firm 1's quantity, columns are firm 2's. Each cell is (firm 1's profit, firm 2's profit).

Grid rebuilt. On the paper this 5×5 table is an unreadable image, so every cell here was recomputed from the course's own formula (MC = 20, P = 140 − Q1 − Q2, profit = (120 − Qi − Qj) × Qi). It was then checked cell by cell against the same table in Presentation 4, and confirmed to reproduce the professor's published elimination order and his answer.

b. 1: the game is dominance solvable, and (40, 40) is the only equilibrium there is.

Never hunt for equilibria in a 5×5 by underlining 25 cells. Shrink it first. The game is symmetric, so whatever you delete for one firm you delete for the other.

1. 60 goes, beaten by 50. Row 60 pays 1800, 1500, 1200, 600, 0. Row 50 pays 2000, 1750, 1500, 1000, 500, which is strictly more in every single column.
2. In the 4×4 that is left, 30 goes, beaten by 35. 1800, 1650, 1500, 1200 against 1925, 1750, 1575, 1225.
3. In the 3×3 left ({35, 40, 50}), 50 goes, beaten by 40. 1750, 1500, 1000 against 1800, 1600, 1200.
4. In the 2×2 left, 35 goes, beaten by 40. 1750, 1575 against 1800, 1600.

One cell survives: (40, 40), paying 1600 each. When elimination of strictly dominated strategies leaves exactly one cell, that cell is the unique Nash equilibrium. There are no mixed ones either, because a mixed equilibrium can never put weight on a strictly dominated strategy. So the count is 1.

Two things to notice. First, why 50 and not 30 kills 60 at step 1: against an opponent playing 30, both 60 and 30 earn exactly 1800, so 30 only weakly dominates 60. Weak dominance is not allowed here, because eliminating weakly dominated strategies can destroy real equilibria. Second, the order in which you eliminate does not change where you end up, as long as every step is strict.

Sanity check on the answer: the continuous version of this exact Cournot game has best reply Qi = (120 − Qj)/2, which crosses itself at 40. The grid was chosen to contain that point.

Data verified. On the paper this 3×3 is a picture, but the professor types the same grid out in full on slide 29 of Presentation 6, so these are his numbers, not a rebuild. Re-solved to confirm they give his published equilibria, (D,L) and (C,R).

d. 6, in the equilibrium (D, L).

Underline player 1's best reply in each column (compare the first numbers, going down):
• column L: 5, 3, 6 → D
• column M: 3, 5, 2 → C
• column R: 2, 4, 3 → C

Underline player 2's best reply in each row (compare the second numbers, going across):
• row U: 9, 5, 6 → L
• row C: 4, 6, 7 → R
• row D: 5, 5, 4 → L and M are tied, so both are best replies

Cells with both underlined: (D, L) paying (6, 5), and (C, R) paying (4, 7). Two pure equilibria. Player 1's payoffs in them are 6 and 4, so his highest is 6.

Also: the question asks for the highest payoff in an equilibrium, not the highest payoff on the board. Player 1's best cell anywhere would be the 6 in (D, L) here. On other papers this is where people grab a big number they can never reach. Always find the equilibria first, then read off.

Question 12Practice exammatrix rebuilt
What is the probability that both players play Left in the Nash equilibrium of the zero-sum game above? (Round to the closest percentage point.)
d. 64%.

Step 1: check that mixing is really needed. Is there a cell that is the smallest in its row and the largest in its column? 1 is the largest in its column but not the smallest in its row (0 is). Try each: no such cell exists. So the equilibrium is mixed.

Step 2: how often does player 2 play Left? Her mixture must leave player 1 indifferent. Call it q.
player 1's row Left: 1q + 0(1−q) = q
player 1's row Right: 0q + 4(1−q) = 4 − 4q
q = 4 − 4q → 5q = 4 → q = 0.8

Step 3: how often does player 1 play Left? Now the other side. His mixture p must leave player 2 indifferent: player 1's payoff must be the same in both columns.
column Left: 1p + 0(1−p) = p   |   column Right: 0p + 4(1−p) = 4 − 4p
p = 0.8

Step 4: the question asks for a cell, not a player. The two players randomise independently, so multiply: 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64 = 64%.

Every wrong option is a step of this calculation. 80 is p on its own, the answer if you forget the question said both. 4% is 0.2 × 0.2, the opposite corner. 20% is 1 − p. 50% is what you get by assuming a fair coin. This pattern repeats on every past paper: read whether the question wants one player's probability or one cell's.
Question 13Practice exammatrix rebuilt
What is the payoff of player 1 in the Nash equilibrium of the same zero-sum game (the matrix above question 12)?
a. 0.8: the value of the game.

You already did the work in question 12. Player 2 plays Left 80% of the time, so player 1's payoff from playing Left is 1 × 0.8 = 0.8. Check it against his other row: 4 × 0.2 = 0.8. The same number, which is exactly what "indifferent" means. And it is the value of the game, the payoff in every Nash equilibrium of a zero-sum game.

This pairing is the most valuable habit on the whole paper. Every past exam ends with two questions on one matrix: a probability, then a payoff. One wrong indifference equation loses you both. That is 14 points, the largest single risk in the room. So when you finish the probability question, do not move on: you are two multiplications away from the next answer.

Where the wrong options come from. 3.2 is 4 × 0.8, the right multiplication with the wrong pairing. 1.6 doubles it. 2.5 is the answer to question 14, sitting here to catch a slipped line.
Question 14Practice exammatrix rebuilt
What is the payoff of player 1 in the Nash equilibrium of the following zero-sum game?
Player 2: LeftPlayer 2: Right
Player 1: Top41
Player 1: Bottom23

One number per cell = player 1's payoff. Player 1 wants it high, player 2 wants it low.

Matrix rebuilt from the algebra in the professor's solution, which pins all four cells: player 1 is indifferent only if player 2 mixes 50/50, player 2 is indifferent only if player 1 plays Top with probability 25%, and the resulting payoff is 0.5×4 + 0.5×1. Re-solved to confirm it gives his answer of 2.5.

d. 2.5.

Step 1: is there a pure equilibrium? Player 1's guaranteed minimum: Top guarantees 1, Bottom guarantees 2, so he can secure 2. Player 2's guarantee: Left can be held to at most 4, Right to at most 3, so she can hold him to 3. Those two numbers differ (2 ≠ 3), so there is no saddle point and mixing is required.

Step 2: find player 2's mixture q (her probability of Left) by making player 1 indifferent.
Top: 4q + 1(1−q) = 1 + 3q   |   Bottom: 2q + 3(1−q) = 3 − q
1 + 3q = 3 − q → 4q = 2 → q = 0.5

Step 3: the value. Put q back into either row: 1 + 3(0.5) = 2.5. Check the other: 3 − 0.5 = 2.5. Same. Player 1's equilibrium payoff is 2.5.

Why this one deserves care. There is not a single zero in this matrix, so the shortcut that works on the usual a,0 / 0,b games (mix b/(a+b), value ab/(a+b)) does not apply. Nothing to memorise: write the two expressions, set them equal, solve. The method is the same every time; only the numbers move.

If you want player 1's own mixture: make player 2 indifferent. Left column gives 4p + 2(1−p) = 2 + 2p; Right gives 1p + 3(1−p) = 3 − 2p; equal at p = 0.25. So he plays Top a quarter of the time. The weaker-looking row is used more, and that is the mixing lesson in one line.
