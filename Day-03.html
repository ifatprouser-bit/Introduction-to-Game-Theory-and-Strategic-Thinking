How to sit it

Copy the real exam conditions as closely as you can. That is what makes a mock worth doing, more than the questions themselves.

  • 90 minutes for 14 questions, about 6 minutes 24 seconds each. Press Start and do not pause.
  • Closed book. No notes, no slides, no other tab. A calculator is allowed, and there is no formula sheet in the real exam.
  • Never leave a blank. There is no stated penalty for a wrong answer, so a guess is free.
  • Two passes. Answer everything you can in the first ~50 minutes, then come back to the three or four heavy ones.
  • Nothing is marked while you work. Clicking an option only records it: no colour, no explanation. You will not know how you are doing until you submit. Same as in the exam room.

When the clock hits zero the paper submits itself. Same as the real thing.

Question 4Practice exam
Which of the following games is a sequential game with perfect information: (I) chess, (II) poker, (III) prisoner's dilemma?
a. Only chess.

"Sequential with perfect information" asks two things at once. First: do the players move one after another? Second: does each player know everything that has already happened? Both have to be true.

• Chess: yes to both. The whole board is visible, and nothing is hidden.
• Poker: sequential, but the information is imperfect. You cannot see your opponent's cards. Taking turns is not enough on its own.
• Prisoner's dilemma: not sequential at all. The two choices are made at the same time. In that game each player has a dominant strategy to defect, and both defecting leaves them both worse off. That is the dilemma.

The trap is (d). Poker feels like the classic strategy game, and it does have turns, so it is easy to wave it through. The hidden cards are what rule it out.

Question 6tree redrawn
Which game is described by the following game tree?
c. Ultimatum.

Read the tree as a story. One player proposes a split of a fixed sum. The other says yes or no. And if he says no, they both get nothing. That "both get zero" branch is the signature of the ultimatum game: the responder's only power is to burn the pie.

How to rule the others out, fast:
• Dictator: the same proposal, but the responder gets no move at all. Here he has two branches, so it is not dictator.
• Prisoner's dilemma: both choose at the same time, and it is drawn as a 2×2 matrix, not a tree.
• Centipede: a long chain of pass-or-take nodes, turn by turn, with the pot growing each time. Two moves is far too short.
• Stag hunt: again a 2×2 game where both choose at the same time, not a tree.

Worth remembering for the behavioural questions. Backward induction says: offer the smallest crumb, and it will be accepted. Real people do not play that way. The most common offer is 50%, the median is about 40%, and offers below 20% are often rejected out of anger.

Question 8Practice exam
Which percentage of people, approximately, choose to cooperate (split) in the high-stake TV prisoner's dilemma game (split or steal) in the show Golden Balls?
c. 50%. Straight recall: "individual players cooperate about 50% of the time".

The point behind the number. Theory says defect. Stealing is dominant, and here it is worth tens of thousands of dollars. Half the contestants split anyway. Raising the stakes barely moves it. So the gap from theory is not only something that happens when the money is small.

Keep these four apart. The exam mixes them:
• Golden Balls cooperation: about 50%
• Lab prisoner's dilemma, first round: about 45%, falling by about 2 points per round
• Dictator game, median gift: about 25%
• Players choosing the 2/3-contest equilibrium of 1: about 10%

That 10% is why option (a) is sitting there. It is a real number from the course, just from a different experiment.
