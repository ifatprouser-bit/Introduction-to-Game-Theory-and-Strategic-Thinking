Warm-up, then the exam

P2P4P5

Thirty minutes this morning. Three warm-up questions, one read of the trap list, one of the tactics. Then close the folder. The exam is at 16:00.

🎧 Before you start under a minute

  • Three warm-up questions. A tree, a grid, one mixing calculation. Do them with a pen.
  • Then the trap list, once, not twice.
  • Then the tactics for the room: the two-pass plan and four habits.
  • Then stop. Nothing new goes in today.

One line for the whole day: you are not learning this morning. You are warming up.

P2 Warm-up 1: one tree ~6 min

First number in each ending is player 1's, second is player 2's. Work backwards from the last node. Read the question carefully.

Purple nodes are player 1's, blue nodes are player 2's. Player 1 moves twice on the upper branch. Green is the answer path. Look at it only after you have solved it.
What is player 2's payoff in the backward-induction equilibrium?
Player 2 gets 2.

Deepest node first. Player 1 chooses between (8, 1) and (3, 6). He reads his own number: 8 beats 3, so he plays x and that node is worth (8, 1).

Player 2's node after A. His choices are a1, worth 2 to him, or a2, which now leads to that (8, 1). He compares 2 against 1 and takes a1. Branch A is worth (5, 2).

Player 2's node after B. b1 gives him 7, b2 gives him 3. He takes b1. Branch B is worth (4, 7).

The root. Player 1 compares his own numbers: A is worth 5, B is worth 4. He plays A. Final outcome (5, 2), so player 2 gets 2.

The trap. 7 is player 2's best number in the whole tree. He cannot get it, because player 1 never goes to that side. If you rush, you might answer 5. That is player 1's payoff, not player 2's. Check which player is asked. That one habit is worth more marks than any formula.

P4 Warm-up 2: one grid ~6 min

First number is player 1's, second is player 2's. Do the full sweep: every column, then every row.

LeftCenterRight
Up3,  57,  24,  4
Middle6,  62,  55,  3
Down1,  48,  76,  6
How many pure Nash equilibria are there? And what is the highest payoff player 1 can get in one?
Two equilibria. Player 1's highest is 8.

Player 1, down each column (first numbers): Left is 3, 6, 1 → Middle. Center is 7, 2, 8 → Down. Right is 4, 5, 6 → Down.

Player 2, across each row (second numbers): Up is 5, 2, 4 → Left. Middle is 6, 5, 3 → Left. Down is 4, 7, 6 → Center.

Both marks: (Middle, Left) = (6, 6) and (Down, Center) = (8, 7). Two of them.

(Down, Right) is the near miss: player 1 is happy there, but player 2 would move across to Center. One happy player is not enough. An equilibrium needs both.

Highest payoff for player 1 in a pure equilibrium: 8. Not 7. That is the biggest number in his Up row, but it sits in no equilibrium at all.

P5 Warm-up 3: one mixed equilibrium ~8 min

Zero-sum: one number per cell, and it is player 1's payoff. Player 1 wants it high, player 2 wants it low.

LeftRight
Top93
Bottom26

Check for a stable cell first. Top's smallest is 3, and 3 is not the biggest in the Right column. Bottom's smallest is 2, and 2 is not the biggest in the Left column. There is no stable cell, so you have to mix.

Find both mixtures and the value of the game. Then: what is the probability that the outcome is (Top, Right), to the nearest percentage point?
Top 40% of the time; Left 30% of the time; value 4.8; P(Top, Right) = 28%.

Player 1's mixture (p for Top) must leave player 2 indifferent, so make the two columns equal:
Left: 9p + 2(1−p) = 2 + 7p  Right: 3p + 6(1−p) = 6 − 3p
2 + 7p = 6 − 3p → 10p = 4 → p = 0.4.

Player 2's mixture (q for Left) must leave player 1 indifferent, so make the two rows equal:
Top: 9q + 3(1−q) = 3 + 6q  Bottom: 2q + 6(1−q) = 6 − 4q
3 + 6q = 6 − 4q → 10q = 3 → q = 0.3.

Value: 2 + 7(0.4) = 4.8. Check from the other side: 3 + 6(0.3) = 4.8. The two agree. That is your proof.

The cell. (Top, Right) is a mismatched pair: Top is player 1's, Right is player 2's. So it is 0.4 × 0.7 = 0.28 = 28%.

Every other option is a mistake you can name: 40% is p on its own, 30% is q on its own, 12% is the matched cell (Top, Left), and 70% is just how often player 2 goes Right. An outcome needs a move from both players, so you always multiply.

⚠️ The trap list: read once ~5 min

Every one of these appears again and again across the six solved papers. Read it slowly, once. Do not re-read it in the exam room.

  1. Which player is asked? The label changes from paper to paper: player 1, player 2, "Player A", "Player B", and sometimes a name like Rowana the row player, or John and Mary. A name is easy to read past. Turn it into "row player" or "column player" before you start, and underline it.
  2. In a tree question, every wrong option is a number that appears in the tree. Usually one of them is a big number on a branch the other player will never let you reach. Solve the tree. Never pick the biggest number you can see.
  3. Counting equilibria: finish the sweep. The options are always 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. One missed cell moves your answer one option along. The wrong answer still looks fine.
  4. "Highest payoff player 1 can get in a pure equilibrium" always offers "the game has no pure equilibrium" as the last option. Check it is really true before you pick it. And read only equilibrium cells, not the whole grid.
  5. The probability of an outcome is p × q, never p. Both single proportions are on the option list, waiting.
  6. The cell they name is often mismatched: one player's row with the other player's column. Read that line twice. Multiplying the matched pair instead is the most expensive slip on the paper.
  7. Read the rounding instruction. It changes between papers: nearest percentage point, two digits after the decimal point, one decimal place. And one paper gives no instruction at all.
  8. 2/3-average: the mean of a uniform range is (min + max) ÷ 2, not 50. With a mixed population, average the whole population first, then take two thirds. The un-averaged number is always offered.
  9. 2/3-average: the equilibrium is 1. The range 13 to 19 is what people play in the lab, not the theory. Read which one the question wants.
  10. Repeated games: read what the opponent forgives before you choose. And "play tit-for-tat" has been offered, but it has never been the answer.
  11. "Name the game" questions hide a non-matrix game in the options: Dictator, Ultimatum. Those are sequential games. A payoff matrix cannot be either of them.
  12. Dominance questions: usually only one player has a dominant strategy. The tempting wrong option adds a strategy for the other player too.
  13. "None of the other answers is correct" has never been the right answer across the six solved papers. So it is unlikely, but not impossible. If you land on it, check your arithmetic once more. If the other four really are wrong, choose it. Never pick it just because you are unsure.

🎯 In the room ~4 min

15 questions (6 minutes each), 90 minutes, closed book, calculator allowed, no formula sheet. Starts at 16:00.

Two passes. Answer everything you can in about the first 50 minutes, then spend the remaining 40 on the three or four heavy ones you left behind.

The heavy questions are usually the pair of zero-sum calculations and one big counting grid. If you meet them on the first pass and they are slow, mark them and move on. A hard question is worth exactly the same as an easy one. Spending six minutes on one, while three easy ones sit unread, is a bad trade.

Never blank
No paper states a penalty for a wrong answer. An empty box always scores zero. A guess can still be right, so never leave a box empty. Before you hand it in, look for gaps. If you have ruled out even one option, your guess has a better chance than a coin flip.
Rounding
Read the instruction in that specific question, every time. Nearest percentage point, one decimal place, two decimals, or nothing at all. It is not the same on every paper. Two questions about the same game can even ask for different rounding.
Which player
Say the name to yourself before you write anything: "I want player 2's number." Then say it again when you pick the option. This costs two seconds. It is the cheapest mark on the paper.
Second pass
Come back to the heavy ones with the easy marks banked and the rest of the clock all yours. You will read them differently. Questions that looked hard on the first pass often look smaller now.

That is everything. You have done four full papers under the clock. You can solve a tree backwards, sweep a grid, and run an indifference equation from the first line to the last. Not because you memorised them, but because you understood why each step is there.

You will not feel ready. Nobody does at breakfast. Feeling ready is not what matters. What matters is being able to do the work. That part is already done.

Eat something. Take a calculator and a pen you like. Get there early, so the walk is calm. Then close this and go.

Good luck, Ifat. Go and enjoy it.
