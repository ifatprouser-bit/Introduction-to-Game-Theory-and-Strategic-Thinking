Question 12025 C finalP6
What is the Salami tactics?
Answer: b. Salami tactics is the thin-slice method. Each slice on its own is too small to be worth a fight, so nobody fights. And the slices add up to the whole salami. It is the exact mirror image of the credibility device moving in small steps: same mechanism, but here you use it to break the other side’s commitment instead of to build your own. (a) is its opposite: one decisive grab is what salami tactics avoids. (c), (d) and (e) are ordinary business or political words. None of them carries the idea of wearing something down slice by slice.
Question 22025 C finalP4
Which of the following games admits multiple (= more than one) pure Nash equilibria?
Answer: a. Battle of the Sexes is a coordination game: both-to-the-opera and both-to-the-football are each a pure equilibrium, and the players disagree about which one they prefer. Two pure equilibria. (b) The prisoner’s dilemma has exactly one, (Defect, Defect), because both players have a dominant strategy. (c) Matching pennies has none in pure strategies. That is the whole point of it, and why it needs a mixed equilibrium. (d) The dictator game has only one player who really decides. One payoff-maximising choice, so one equilibrium. Since (a) works, (e) fails.
Question 32025 C finalP3
In a finitely repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma played for 10 rounds, one player adopts the following strategy: cooperate in round 1, play tit-for-tat in rounds 2–8 (i.e., play the action played by the opponent in the previous round), and defect in rounds 9–10. What is a best response strategy for the opponent?
Answer: a. Ask one question only: which of my rounds actually change his behaviour? His tit-for-tat covers rounds 2–8, and it copies what I did the round before. So my rounds 1 to 7 steer him. My rounds 8, 9 and 10 steer nothing, because he defects in 9 and 10 no matter what I do, and there is no round 11. So rounds 8, 9 and 10 are free defections: three of them, not two.

This is the off-by-one trap in its purest form. Option (c) is the answer almost everybody gives: “he defects from 9, so I defect from 9”. It throws away round 8 for nothing. Option (b) defects in round 1, which buys one big payoff but makes him defect in round 2, and that trade is a loss. Scoring all five options with the standard prisoner’s dilemma values (temptation 5, reward 3, punishment 1, sucker 0): (a) = 28, (c) = 26, (b) = 25, (e) = 25, (d) = 24. A brute-force search over all 1,024 possible ten-round strategies confirms (a) is the true best response.
Question 42025 C finalP4
What is the payoff of Player 2 in the unique pure equilibrium of the game?
Player 2
Player 1LeftCenterRight
Up9, 84, 77, 9
Middle8, 35, 48, 1
Down10, 62, 77, 6

First number = player 1 (row), second number = player 2 (column).

Answer: a, 4. Underline player 1’s best reply in each column: Left → Down (10), Center → Middle (5), Right → Middle (8). Underline player 2’s best reply in each row: Up → Right (9), Middle → Center (4), Down → Center (7). The only cell with both underlined is (Middle, Center) = (5, 4). Player 2 gets 4.
The circulated solution is wrong here, and it is worth two minutes of your time. It says the equilibrium is (Up, Center) and that player 1 gets 7. It is not. (Up, Center) is not an equilibrium at all: from there player 1 would move to Middle (5 beats 4) and player 2 would move to Right (9 beats 7). The true cell is (Middle, Center), holding (5, 4).

How did the author get 7 and 4? In the original PDF each cell prints two numbers, and in this exam’s files the number printed first is player 2’s and the second is player 1’s. That is the reverse of the usual convention. The cell that prints as “7 then 4” is (Up, Center), and that is where the pair 7 and 4 came from. But (Up, Center) is not an equilibrium under either reading of the grid. So both halves of that sentence are wrong: the cell, and player 1’s 7. Only the final answer of 4 survives, and it survives by accident. This is the single most expensive habit on the whole paper: always check which number belongs to which player before you underline anything.
Question 52025 C finalP2
Assume that you are Player 2 in the 21-Flags game (a 2-player game; sequential play; each player has to remove in her turn 1–3 flags; the player to remove the last flag wins). Player 1 has begun the game by removing 2 of the 21 flags (so now there are 19 remaining flags). How many flags should you remove in order to maximize the probability that you win the game?
Answer: c, 3. The whole game is one rule: leave your opponent a multiple of 4. Whatever he then takes (1, 2 or 3), you take what makes it up to 4. So the pile drops 4 every round, and he is the one facing 4, then 0. From 19 flags: 19 − 3 = 16, and 16 is a multiple of 4. Taking 1 leaves 18, taking 2 leaves 17. Both of those hand him the winning position. Player 1 made the mistake first: 21 is not a multiple of 4, and after removing 2 he left 19, which is not one either. So the game is now yours to lose. Checked by backward induction over every position, 3 is the only winning move from 19.
Question 62025 C finalP1P4
Assume that you are playing the 2/3-average competition with 200 other players. In this game, each player chooses an integer between 1 and 100. The players whose chosen number is closest to 2/3 of the average of all chosen numbers win. What would be your best response if the population is such that: (1) half of the players choose a number that is a best response against uniform play on {1, …, 100}, and (2) the remaining half choose a number that is the best response to the number chosen by the first half?
Answer: b, 19. Three steps. Do not skip the middle one.
1. Uniform play on {1, …, 100} has mean (1 + 100) / 2 = 50.5, not 50. Two-thirds of 50.5 = 33.67, so the first half choose 34.
2. The second half best-respond to 34: two-thirds of 34 = 22.67, so they choose 23.
3. Now average the population first, then take two-thirds: (34 + 23) / 2 = 28.5, and two-thirds of 28.5 = 19 exactly.

Now look at where the four wrong options come from. 33 is two-thirds of 50. That is step 1 with the mean rounded to 50 instead of 50.5, the single most common slip here. 22 is two-thirds of 33, the same slip carried into step 2. 27 is that same slipped chain’s population average, (33 + 22) / 2 = 27.5, when you stop before the final multiplication. That is the “forgot the two-thirds” answer. And 15 is one round too far: two-thirds of 23 ≈ 15.3, which is what you get if you keep chaining down instead of stopping at the population you were actually given. Every wrong option is a real calculation stopped one step early or run one step late. The order matters too: average first, multiply second. Taking two-thirds of each group and then averaging happens to give the same number here, but it will not always.
Question 72025 C wordingtree redrawnP2
What will be the payoff of player 2 (= II) in the backward-induction equilibrium of the following three-player sequential game? (The first number in each leaf is the payoff for player 1 (= I), the second the payoff for player 2 (= II), and the third the payoff for player 3 (= III); the roman numeral in each node shows which of the three players moves at that node.)

Leaves read (player I, player II, player III).

Answer: e, 11. Start at the deepest node and never look forward.
• Bottom node (II): c1 gives II 5, c2 gives II 0. II plays c1 → (2, 5, 9).
• Right node (III): b1 gives III 7, b2 now leads to (2, 5, 9) which gives III 9. III plays b2 → (2, 5, 9).
• Left node (II): a1 gives II 11, a2 gives II 2. II plays a1 → (6, 11, 3).
• Root (I): L leads to (6, 11, 3) giving I 6; R leads to (2, 5, 9) giving I 2. I plays L.
The path is L then a1, and player II collects 11.

Notice what the wrong options are: 0, 2, 5 and 8 are all payoffs player II gets somewhere in this tree. That is how every tree question on every paper is built. Each wrong option is a real number lifted from a leaf you did not reach. Reading a number off the picture is not solving it. Also watch the third player. III’s choice at b2 looks kind to II, because it gives II 5 rather than 8. But III is not being kind. III simply prefers 9 to 7. Each node maximises only its own player’s number.
Note on this question. The tree in the original PDF is a picture with no text layer, so its exact shape cannot be recovered. The tree above is a reconstruction. It matches everything the official solution states: three players, roman-numeral nodes, backward induction, and player II receiving 11. All four wrong options appear as real player-II payoffs, as on the real paper. It matches the published answer of 11 exactly.

Question 82025 C finalP5
What is the probability that the outcome of the zero-sum game above is (Down, Left) in the mixed Nash equilibrium of this game? (Rounded to the nearest percentage point.)
Answer: c, 36%. Two indifference equations, then one multiplication.

Find q = P(Left), the mix that makes player 1 indifferent.
Top pays 0·q + 30·(1 − q) = 30 − 30q. Down pays 20·q + 0·(1 − q) = 20q.
30 − 30q = 20q → 50q = 30 → q = 0.6, so Left 60%, Right 40%.

Find p = P(Top), the mix that makes player 2 indifferent. Player 2’s payoffs are the negatives.
Left pays −20(1 − p). Right pays −30p.
20(1 − p) = 30p → 50p = 20 → p = 0.4, so Top 40%, Down 60%.

The question asks for a cell, not a strategy: P(Down, Left) = P(Down) × P(Left) = 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.36 = 36%.
Why 24% is on the paper. Five of the seven past zero-sum matrices have zeros on the main diagonal, shape a,0 / 0,b, and for those the remembered shortcut p = q = b/(a+b) works. This paper flips the zeros onto the anti-diagonal. Now the shortcut crosses the probabilities over. It hands you 0.4 × 0.6 = 24%, which is printed as option (b). The examiner put it there on purpose. The defence is never the shortcut: write the two indifference equations from the matrix in front of you, every time. Also check which cell is asked. Here it is (Down, Left), so you need P(Down) and P(Left), and P(Down) = 1 − p, not p. Option (d) 40% and (e) 60% are single probabilities, not cells. Option (a) 16% is 0.4 × 0.4.
Question 92025 C finalP5
What is the expected payoff of Player 1 in the mixed Nash equilibrium of the zero-sum game above?
Answer: b, 12. The whole point of the indifference method is that once you have q, both of player 1’s rows pay the same. So you can use either one:
Top: 30 × (1 − q) = 30 × 0.4 = 12.
Down: 20 × q = 20 × 0.6 = 12. ✓
They agree, which is your free check that q is right. That number, 12, is the value of the game. Player 2’s expected payoff is −12, because the game is zero-sum.

Q8 and Q9 sit on the same matrix and the same two equations. One slip in the indifference algebra loses both. That is 14 marks, the biggest single block of risk on the paper. So spend the extra thirty seconds: solve the mix once, write p and q down on your draft paper, and use them for both questions.

Note also: this paper gives no rounding instruction on Q9, where Q8 says “nearest percentage point”. Other papers say “two digits after the decimal point” or “one decimal place”. Read the instruction on the paper in front of you, because it changes.
Question 102025 C wordinggrid rebuiltP4
How many pure Nash equilibria exist in the following game (where the first number in each cell describes the payoff of player 1, the row player, and the second number describes the payoff of player 2, the column player)?
Player 2
Player 1DEF
A2, 45, 31, 2
B1, 53, 16, 4
C0, 64, 22, 3
Answer: b, 1. Do it mechanically, nine cells, no shortcuts.
Player 1’s best reply down each column: D → A (2 beats 1 and 0); E → A (5); F → B (6).
Player 2’s best reply across each row: A → D (4 beats 3 and 2); B → D (5); C → D (6).
Both underlined in the same cell: only (A, D) = (2, 4). That is one pure Nash equilibrium.

Check the near-misses, because this is where counting questions are won and lost. (A, E) has player 1’s underline but not player 2’s, because from row A she would move to D. (B, F) has player 1’s underline but not player 2’s. One underline is never enough. You need both in the same cell. The options run 0 to 4, so a single missed or a single over-counted cell changes your answer.

Two cells have both: (Up, Left) = (1, 2) and (Low, Right) = (7, 4). The question asks for the highest Rowana can get in an equilibrium, so the answer is 7.

Two traps in one question. First, it asks for the highest in an equilibrium, not the highest in the matrix, and not the equilibrium that is best for player 2. Find every equilibrium, then read off Rowana’s number. Second, 5 is Rowana’s payoff at (Middle, Right), a big number in a cell that is not an equilibrium at all. The strongest argument on this page for solving again instead of remembering. This exact question appears word for word on another paper. The same stem: “What is the highest payoff that Rowana (player 1, the row player) can obtain in a pure Nash equilibrium?” The same options: 1, 2, 5, 7. It is question 11 on the 2024 A paper. There the answer is 1, because that game has a single equilibrium at (Up, Left). Here it is 7. Same words, different grid, different answer. If you recognise a question in the exam room and let that recognition pick your answer, this is exactly where it costs you seven marks. Recognise the type, then solve the matrix in front of you.

Question 122025 C finalP4
Which of the following best describes a Nash equilibrium in game theory?
Answer: e. The two load-bearing words are no player and unchanged. Everyone is already playing a best reply to what everyone else is doing, so nobody gains by moving alone. Nothing is said about the outcome being good.

(a) confuses equilibrium with Pareto efficiency. The prisoner’s dilemma is the standing counterexample: (Defect, Defect) is the equilibrium and both players hate it. (b) confuses it with cooperation. (c) says the exact opposite of the definition, and it is the option most people click when they read too fast. (d) describes random play, not equilibrium. Definition questions are free marks, so read all five options to the end before choosing.
Question 132025 C finalP3
What is a dominated strategy?
Answer: b. Dominated means beaten in every single column. Some other strategy does better against everything the opponent could do. No condition, no exception. That is why a rational player never plays one, and why you can cross it off the matrix.

Note the mirror: (a) describes a dominant strategy, the opposite, and it is placed first on purpose. (c) is close to a maximin idea, not domination. (d) defines a best response. The giveaway is “to at least one”, where domination needs all. (e) describes a strategy that is neither dominant nor dominated. Watch for the words every / regardless versus at least one / sometimes: that single word decides these questions.
Question 142025 C finalP3P4
Which of the following games has two pure equilibria, where one of them is Pareto better than the other equilibrium (i.e., it yields both players a higher payoff than what they get in the other pure equilibrium)?
Answer: d, Stag hunt. Two pure equilibria, (Stag, Stag) and (Hare, Hare), and both players prefer the same one: hunting the stag together pays each of them more than each hunting a hare alone. That is what Pareto better means: better for everyone at once, with no argument about which one to pick. The only obstacle is trust. Hare is safe; Stag needs your partner to show up.

This is the clean line between the coordination games. In stag hunt the two equilibria are ranked the same way by both players. In Battle of the Sexes (b) and in Chicken (c) there are also two pure equilibria, but each player prefers a different one. So neither is Pareto better, and the conflict is about which one to land on. (a) The prisoner’s dilemma has only one pure equilibrium, so it cannot qualify no matter how bad that equilibrium is.

Worth knowing: this question also appears word for word as question 4 on the 2024 B paper. Definition and archetype questions repeat across years far more than calculations do. That makes the five archetypes (Day 2) some of the cheapest marks on the paper.
