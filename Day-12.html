Question 12025 C finalP6
What is the Salami tactics?
Answer: b. Salami tactics is the thin-slice method. You take one thin slice at a time. Each slice is too small to be worth a fight, so nobody fights. But the slices add up to the whole salami.

It is the mirror image of the credibility device moving in small steps. Same mechanism. The difference is what you point it at: here you use small steps to break the other side’s commitment (the position they have locked themselves into), not to build your own.

(a) is the opposite. One decisive grab is exactly what salami tactics avoids. (c), (d) and (e) are ordinary business or political phrases. None of them carries the idea of wearing something down slice by slice.
Question 22025 C finalP4
Which of the following games admits multiple (= more than one) pure Nash equilibria?
Answer: a. First, the term. A pure Nash equilibrium is a pair of choices, one per player, where neither player gains by changing on their own. “Pure” means each player picks one action, not a random mix.

Battle of the Sexes is a coordination game. Both go to the opera, or both go to the football. Each of those is a pure Nash equilibrium. The players disagree about which one they prefer, but neither wants to walk off alone. So: two pure equilibria.

(b) The prisoner’s dilemma has exactly one, (Defect, Defect). Both players have a dominant strategy, which is a move that is best no matter what the other does. (c) Matching pennies has none in pure strategies. That is the whole point of it, and it is why the game needs a mixed equilibrium, where each player chooses at random. (d) The dictator game has only one player who really decides. One best choice for that player, so one equilibrium. Since (a) works, (e) fails.

Question 42025 C finalP4
What is the payoff of Player 2 in the unique pure equilibrium of the game?
Player 2
Player 1LeftCenterRight
Up9, 84, 77, 9
Middle8, 35, 48, 1
Down10, 62, 77, 6

First number = player 1 (row), second number = player 2 (column).

Answer: a, 4.
The circulated solution is wrong on this question. Give it two minutes.

It says the equilibrium is (Up, Center), and that player 1 gets 7. Both parts are wrong.

First: (Up, Center) is not an equilibrium. From that cell player 1 would move to Middle, because 5 beats 4. And player 2 would move to Right, because 9 beats 7. Both players want to leave, so it cannot be an equilibrium. The true cell is (Middle, Center), which holds (5, 4).

Second: so where did the numbers 7 and 4 come from? In the original PDF every cell prints its two payoffs one above the other. In this exam’s files the first printed number is player 2’s, and the second is player 1’s. That is the reverse of the usual order. The cell that prints as “7 then 4” is (Up, Center), and that is where the author’s 7 and 4 came from. But under that printing rule the 7 is player 2’s payoff. The author read it as player 1’s.

Third: read the grid either way round, and (Up, Center) still is not an equilibrium. So the solution gets two things wrong: the cell it names, and the 7 it gives to player 1. Only its final answer, 4, is right, and it is right by accident.

This is the most expensive habit on the whole paper. Before you underline anything, check which number belongs to which player.

Question 122025 C finalP4
Which of the following best describes a Nash equilibrium in game theory?
Answer: e. Two phrases carry the whole definition: no player, and while the others stay unchanged. Everyone is already playing a best reply to what everyone else is doing. So nobody gains by moving alone. Notice that nothing is said about the outcome being good.

(a) confuses equilibrium with efficiency, meaning an outcome that is good for everyone. The prisoner’s dilemma is the standing counterexample: (Defect, Defect) is the equilibrium, and both players hate it. (b) confuses equilibrium with cooperation. (c) says the exact opposite of the definition. It is the option most people click when they read too fast. (d) describes random play, not equilibrium. Definition questions are free marks, so read all five options to the end before you choose.
Question 132025 C finalP3
What is a dominated strategy?
Answer: b. A dominated strategy is one that is beaten in every single column. Some other strategy of yours does better against everything the opponent could do. No condition, no exception. That is why a rational player never plays one, and why you can cross it off the matrix.

Note the mirror. (a) describes a dominant strategy, which is the opposite, and it is placed first on purpose. (c) is close to a maximin idea (protect yourself against the worst case), not domination. (d) defines a best response. The giveaway is “to at least one”, where domination needs all. (e) describes a strategy that is neither dominant nor dominated.

Watch the words: every / regardless against at least one / sometimes. That single word decides these questions.
Question 142025 C finalP3P4
Which of the following games has two pure equilibria, where one of them is Pareto better than the other equilibrium (i.e., it yields both players a higher payoff than what they get in the other pure equilibrium)?
Answer: d, Stag hunt. It has two pure equilibria, (Stag, Stag) and (Hare, Hare). And both players prefer the same one: hunting the stag together pays each of them more than each hunting a hare alone. That is what Pareto better means. Better for everyone at once, so there is no argument about which one to pick. The only obstacle is trust. Hare is safe. Stag needs your partner to show up.

This is the clean line between the coordination games. In stag hunt, both players rank the two equilibria the same way. In Battle of the Sexes (b) and in Chicken (c) there are also two pure equilibria, but each player prefers a different one. So neither equilibrium is Pareto better, and the fight is about which one to land on. (a) The prisoner’s dilemma has only one pure equilibrium, so it cannot qualify, however bad that equilibrium is.

Worth knowing: this question also appears word for word as question 4 on the 2024 B paper. Definition and archetype questions repeat across years far more than calculations do. That makes the five archetypes (Day 2) some of the cheapest marks on the paper.
