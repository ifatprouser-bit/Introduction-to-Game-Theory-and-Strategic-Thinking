Making strategies credible

P6

No calculation anywhere today. This is a recall day: eight names, eight pictures. Commitment is worth at least one question out of 14, and sometimes two. It was first taught in 2025, so only the three 2025 papers carry these questions.

⏸ Guess first

You own the only bakery in a small town. Someone is thinking of opening a second one across the road. You put a sign in your window: “Open here and I will drop my prices until neither of us earns a shekel.”

A price war really would lose you money. You would lose more than if you simply shared the town with her. Your neighbour knows this too.

Does the sign keep her out?

Take 20 seconds. Commit to an answer before you read on.

🎧 Why a threat is not believed ~1 min

A quick map of what you'll learn today. Each section below also has its own short audio.

  • Credible threat: a threat you really would carry out when the moment comes. A non-credible threat is one you would not carry out. Backward induction, which means solving a game tree from the last move backwards, sees straight through it. So it changes nothing.
  • Commitment: tying your own hands on purpose, before the other player chooses.
  • Strategic move: changing the game before the other player chooses, so a better ending becomes possible. You usually do it by taking away one of your own options.
  • The eight devices: contracts, reputation, cutting off communication, burning bridges behind you, leaving the outcome beyond your control, moving in small steps, teamwork, mandated negotiating agents. Learn them in that order.
  • Salami tactics: the same small-steps idea turned around and used to break somebody else's commitment, one slice at a time.

One line to hold onto: you become believable by giving up your own freedom to change your mind.

The problem: a threat nobody believes P6P2

🎧 Listen to this section

The whole lecture starts from one hard fact: saying a thing does not make it true. Presentation 6 opens with the monopolist's entry game. A monopolist is the only firm in a market. The game is only two moves long.

A competitor is thinking of entering the market. The monopolist has said he will start a price war against anyone who comes in. Here is the tree.

The green path is the backward-induction path. The red branch is the threat: it exists in the tree, and nobody ever walks it.

Solve it from the end, as always. To accommodate means to share the market quietly. Stand at the monopolist's node, with the competitor already inside. Fighting pays the monopolist −1. Accommodating pays 2. So the monopolist accommodates. Not out of kindness. At that moment, fighting is simply the worse of two bad options.

The competitor can see that node too. Entering gives her 2. Staying out gives her 0. So she enters. The monopolist ends on 2 when he wanted 4. The sign in the window changed nothing at all.

The test for a threat is never “is it frightening?” It is “would I really carry it out when the moment came?” If not, it is not a threat. It is noise, and backward induction sees straight through it.

Worked example

In the entry game above, the monopolist has said in public that he will fight anyone who enters. Solve the game by backward induction. What does each player really do? What does the monopolist get? And what does he lose by having a threat nobody believes?
1
Go to the last decision node, which is the monopolist's.
Compare only the two endings that follow it: Accommodate gives him 2, Fight gives him −1.
2 > −1 → he accommodates
2
Replace that whole node by what will happen there.
The branch marked “Enter” now leads, in practice, to (2, 2). The fight ending drops out of the competitor's thinking. Not because it is impossible, but because it will never be chosen.
3
Now the competitor's node.
She compares her own numbers only. Enter gives her 2, and Stay out gives her 0.
2 > 0 → she enters
4
Read the path off the tree.
Enter, then Accommodate. Outcome (2, 2). Compare with the ending the monopolist was aiming at, (4, 0).
✓ The competitor enters and the monopolist accommodates. He gets 2 instead of the 4 he wanted. The threat cost him nothing to make and earned him nothing, because it was never going to be carried out.
Notice the shape of the failure. The monopolist's problem is not that his threat is too small. It is that he has too much freedom. On the day, he is still free to choose the sensible thing, and everybody knows it. Every device in this lecture attacks that freedom.
A landlord tells a new tenant: “pay one day late and I evict you.” Evicting means a court fee and two months with the flat empty. Accepting the late rent costs the landlord nothing at all. The tenant knows both facts.

Is the threat credible? And what exactly is the landlord's problem?
Not credible. Put yourself at the landlord's node, one day after the rent was due. Evicting is worse for him than accepting the money. So he accepts. The tenant looks ahead and already knows that, so the threat does not change her behaviour.

His problem is not the size of the threat. It is his own freedom. He is still free to be reasonable on the day. To fix it he must remove that freedom in advance. One way is a signed clause with an automatic penalty: a contract, enforced by someone else. Another is to hand the flat to a letting agency whose staff are not allowed to let anything go: mandated negotiating agents.

The same shape, at the highest stakes, and the way out P6P2

🎧 Listen to this section

The second example in Presentation 6 is the cold war in Europe. The cold war was the long standoff between the USA and the Soviet Union, with no direct fighting between them. The Soviet Union had the stronger army on the ground. The American answer was a promise: invade a western country, and we go nuclear.

The same shape as the bakery. Start at the USA's node: −1 beats −10, so it backs down. The Soviet Union sees that, and 1 beats 0, so it invades.

Now look at what did not save the threat. Its size. The nuclear ending is by far the worst thing anywhere in that tree, and it is still not believed. The side doing the punishing is also being punished. Terrifying is not the same as credible.

So stop trying to make the threat bigger. Change the game instead. Presentation 6 calls this a strategic move: something you do now, before the other player chooses, that changes the tree in your favour. In practice it almost always means making one of your own branches worse.

One leaf moved, and the whole solution flips. Backing down now costs more than fighting, so the USA would fight. The Soviet Union sees that and stays home, so the fight never happens.
A strategic move works by making your own option worse, in advance, in a way the other side can see. Two conditions: it has to be done before he chooses, and it has to be visible to him. A secret commitment deters nobody.

Worked example

The bakery again, with the original numbers: (Enter, Accommodate) pays the two of you 2 and 2; (Enter, Fight) pays −1 and −1; Stay out pays you 4 and her 0. Before she decides, you sign contracts with your regular customers. You promise that if a rival ever opens, you will beat their price all year. If you do not, you must pay each customer a penalty. Now quietly sharing the town costs you −2 instead of 2.

Does she still enter?
1
Redraw only the leaf the contract touched.
Accommodate now pays you −2. Fight still pays you −1. Nothing else in the tree changed.
2
Re-solve your node.
−1 > −2 → you fight The threat has become the thing you would really do. It is now credible, and it became credible without becoming any bigger.
3
Re-solve her node with that in mind.
Entering now leads to the war ending, which pays her −1. Staying out pays her 0.
0 > −1 → she stays out
4
Read off your own payoff.
She stays out, so you land on 4, the ending you wanted from the start.
✓ She stays out, and you get 4 instead of 2. Read that twice: you made one of your own endings worse on purpose, and ended up better off. And because she stays out, you never actually pay the penalty. The device works exactly by never being used.
Same bakery, but you sign the contracts in secret, planning to reveal them the day she opens. Does the device still work?
No. A commitment device changes the other player's behaviour only through what she believes when she chooses. If she cannot see the contract at the moment she is deciding, her tree is the old tree, and she enters exactly as before.

You will then really fight, because the contract is real. So you both land on −1, which is worse for you than the 2 you would have had with no contract at all. An invisible commitment is not a commitment. It is just a wound you gave yourself. Make it visible and impossible to undo, or do not bother.
Why does the professor say the nuclear threat fails even though it is the most frightening thing in the tree? Answer in one sentence, as if you were explaining it to a friend.
Something like: “because carrying it out would also destroy the side making the threat, so at the moment of truth they would not do it, and the other side knows that in advance.”

The size of a punishment does not decide whether it is credible. What matters is only the comparison at your own node, between carrying it out and not carrying it out. A threat that hurts you more than it helps you is empty, however loud it is.

The eight devices: eight names, eight pictures

🎧 Listen to this section

Every device below is an answer to one question: how do I make myself unable to back down? Learn them in the professor's order. The exam question is always “here is a story, which one is it?” A list in a fixed order is much easier to search than a pile.

The memory board. Cover the right-hand words and say the eight names down the columns; then cover the names and say what each picture is.

Now the same eight, with what each one actually does. Every device buys credibility by taking something away from you. The last column is the one to understand, because it is the same idea eight times over.

DeviceThe pictureWhat it takes away from you
1ContractsYou sign a deal that somebody outside can enforce against you. NATO Article 5: an attack on one member is an attack on all. Also the kitchen contract, where each payment is tied to the work finished. Also the monopolist's promise to customers to beat any rival's price.Your right to decide on the day. Somebody outside the deal now carries out the punishment. It no longer depends on how you feel at the time.
2ReputationYou stake your good name on doing what you said. In the Berlin crisis, the Soviet side pressed the West to leave the city. Kennedy answered in public: “if we are not true to our word there, all that we have achieved in collective security, which relies on these words, will mean nothing.”The cheap way out. Backing down once would cost you your name in every future deal, so keeping your word is now your better choice.
3Cutting off communicationYou make yourself impossible to reach, so nobody can talk you out of it. In the film Dr. Strangelove, a base commander sends the bombers and then destroys the radio. The slides’ extreme example is a last will and testament: the person who wrote it cannot be argued with at all.Your ability to be talked out of it. No message can reach you, so the deal cannot be re-opened and the threat runs on by itself.
4Burning bridges behind youYou destroy your own way back, so the only way left is forward. William the Conqueror burned his own ships after landing in England (1066). Hernán Cortés burned his ships after landing in Mexico (1519). In the film The Hunt for Red October, a submarine captain posts a letter to his admiral before he sails, saying he is changing sides. His own navy will now hunt him, so turning back would kill him. Sun Tzu, an old Chinese writer on war, gives the other side of the idea: when you surround an enemy, leave him a way out.Your retreat. There is no road back, so everybody can see that fighting is the only thing left for you to do.
5Leaving the outcome beyond your controlYou hand the decision to a machine or to chance, so nobody can choose on the day. Dr. Strangelove again: the doomsday machine answers automatically and cannot be switched off. The real version is missiles held ready to launch during a crisis. In the film L.A. Confidential, a detective leaves one bullet in a revolver and keeps pulling the trigger at a suspect.Your freedom to choose, on purpose. The response is no longer a human decision, so there is nobody left to argue with.
6Moving in small stepsYou cut one big act of trust into many small ones. The kitchen remodelling is paid day by day, or week by week, as each part of the work is finished. Neither side ever has much at risk.The size of the promise. Each small promise is cheap to keep, so each one is easy to believe.
7TeamworkYou join a group that punishes any member who gives in. In the Roman army, running away was punished by death. Failing to kill a man who ran was also punished by death. Also Alcoholics Anonymous, a group for people trying to stop drinking, where breaking your promise costs you your standing with everyone else.Your privacy. The group is watching, and looking the other way is punished too, so nobody can quietly let it go.
8Mandated negotiating agentsYou send somebody to talk who has no power to agree. The union leader takes a narrow mandate, which is a public limit set by his own members, and states it openly. Also sports agents, and the shop assistant who is simply not allowed to change the price.Your power to give in. The person in the room really cannot say yes, so “no” becomes believable.
Ask three questions of any story: who is trying to be believed? what freedom did they give up? and who or what makes that stick? The answer to the second question names the device almost every time.

Worked example

Before price talks with a large supplier, a company's board passes a written rule. The chief executive may not sign any deal above a stated price without a full vote of the shareholders. The board then prints the rule in a press release, so everyone can read it. Which of the eight devices is this, and why does it work?
1
Who is trying to be believed, and of what?
The company. It wants the supplier to believe it will walk away rather than pay more than the stated price.
2
Why would a plain promise fail?
Because on the day, paying a little more still beats having no deal at all. The supplier knows that, so “this is our final price” is exactly the non-credible threat from the first section.
3
What freedom was given up?
The negotiator's power to give in. The chief executive simply cannot agree in the room. The press release makes that visible to the other side before they choose.
4
Match it against the list.
It is not a contract with the supplier, because no agreement between the two sides exists yet. It is not burning bridges, because the company can still walk away, which is the whole point. It is the negotiator who has been tied down.
✓ Mandated negotiating agents (device 8). It works exactly the way the union leader's public mandate works: the negotiator is made weaker on purpose, and that weakness is what makes the refusal believable.
A hospital's night pharmacy is robbed again and again. The manager installs a time-lock safe. Once closed, it cannot be opened by anybody, including the staff, until morning. A large sign on the door says so.

Which device, and what is the sign for?
Leaving the outcome beyond your control (device 5). “We won't open the safe” is not credible when a robber is standing in front of a frightened member of staff. On the night, opening it is obviously better than being hurt. The time lock takes the choice away from the staff completely, so there is nothing left to threaten out of them.

The sign is not decoration. A commitment only changes behaviour through what the other side believes when they choose. A secret time lock protects the drugs, but it does not stop the robbery.
Two strangers agree to swap a second-hand car for cash. Neither trusts the other to hand over first, so they use a lawyer. She holds both the money and the keys, and she releases each one only when both have arrived.

Which device is this, and which other device could have solved the same problem without a lawyer?
Contracts (device 1). This one shows why the slides make such a fuss about third-party enforcement. The lawyer is the third party. Without someone outside the deal to hold the stakes, the agreement is only two promises facing each other.

The other answer is moving in small steps (device 6): swap in pieces. Part of the cash, then the keys, then the rest, then the paperwork. Neither side ever risks much. That is the same cure the slides give to the homeowner who does not trust the builder.

A third possibility, if one of them is a dealer with a name to protect: reputation (device 2). The slides say it outright. A strong reputation can remove the need for a contract.

The small print, and salami tactics

🎧 Listen to this section

The slides do not stop at the list. Each device comes with a catch, and the exam often likes the catch more than the device. The slides name five of them.

DeviceThe catch the slides flag
ContractsA contract can never cover everything, and it must survive being re-opened. If on the day both sides would rather tear it up, it was never a commitment. It also needs third-party enforcement. Where courts are slow or corrupt, people turn to private judges or worse.
ReputationA strong reputation can replace a contract completely. That is a strength, but it means the reputation itself is what you are betting, and one public climb-down spends it.
Cutting off communicationYou pay twice. You lose flexibility, because you cannot react to news you never hear. And you lose the ability to check, because you cannot even confirm your message arrived.
Beyond your controlA delicate balance. Strong enough to deter, but not so strong that the false alarm, which will come sooner or later, destroys everything. Taking away your own choice is what makes it work and what makes it dangerous.
Moving in small stepsWorks only when there is no clear final step. As soon as both sides can see the last one coming, they reason backwards from it, and the trust unravels from the end. It works better when there is a chance of future business.
The pattern behind all five: a device buys credibility with your own flexibility. The more of it you sell, the more believable you are. And the worse it goes when the world does something you did not plan for.

Renegotiation-proofness: the catch worth understanding properly

To renegotiate means to re-open the deal and agree to change it or cancel it. Of those five, the first is the one the slides explain in detail. A contract you would both happily tear up is not a commitment at all. The professor's example is a self-control contract: anyone who photographs Mr. X eating unhealthy food collects a large reward. It works. Then Mr. X, standing in front of the cake, offers the photographer a share of the reward to look away. Both are better off tearing the contract up, so both do. The commitment disappears exactly when it was needed.

Compare the version the slides say does hold. Some rehabilitation centres, which are clinics that help people stop using drugs, ask a patient to write a damaging letter about himself in advance. The letter is published if he fails a random drug test. Why can that one not be bought off? The answer has two parts, and both are worth learning:

If the person holding the letter……then
agrees to renegotiate and suppress itthey are fired. So taking the bribe costs them their job. Re-opening the deal is not in their interest either.
is not fired for suppressing itthe centre's reputation is lost, and with it the whole scheme's value for every future patient. So the centre will fire them.

Read those two rows together and you have the general recipe: a contract survives being re-opened when somebody would lose by agreeing to tear it up. Notice what is holding it together in the second row. It is reputation, doing the job of an enforcer. That is the same swap the slides make for device 2.

Test any contract with one question: on the day, would both sides rather cancel it? If yes, it commits nobody. A contract only binds you if tearing it up would hurt somebody whose agreement is needed to tear it up.

Salami tactics: the same idea, running backwards

A salami is a large sausage. Nobody could eat one whole, but anybody can eat it one thin slice at a time. That is the picture behind the name. Moving in small steps builds a commitment slowly. Run the same idea the other way and you can take somebody else's commitment apart. The professor's example is a child told not to go in the water.

Not one of those steps is worth a fight on its own. That is exactly what makes the last one possible.

Each slice is too small to be worth the argument it would cause, so the parent lets it go. And the next slice starts from the new line, not the old one. Soon the parent is shouting at a child who is swimming out of sight. The slides call this salami tactics. The cold-war example is a sketch from Yes Prime Minister, a British television comedy about government.

Moving in small steps builds your own commitment, a little at a time. Salami tactics cuts away somebody else's commitment, in slices so thin that no single slice is worth a fight. Same tool. Opposite direction. The exam loves this pair, so check who is building and who is cutting.

Worked example

A homeowner and a builder agree that a kitchen will be paid for in five equal stages, one after each part of the work is finished. Presentation 6 says this is a good arrangement, but it also warns about one weak point. Which stage is the fragile one? Why? And what does the lecture suggest doing about it?
1
Why the arrangement works at all.
Neither side ever has much at risk. If the builder disappears, the homeowner has lost one stage's money; if the homeowner refuses to pay, the builder has lost one stage's work. Small stakes make trust cheap.
2
Find the fragile stage using backward induction.
Go to the last stage. After it there is nothing: no more payments, no more work, no future to lose. So the builder has no reason to do it well, and the homeowner has no reason to pay.
3
Let the reasoning roll backwards.
Knowing the last stage will go badly, the second-to-last stage has nothing worth protecting either, and so on. The trust comes undone from the end backwards. This is the same unravelling as in the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma from Day 8, the one played a known number of times.
4
Apply the lecture's remedy.
The slides say small steps work best when there is no clear final step, and when there is a chance of future business.
✓ The last stage is the fragile one, because backward induction starts there and works its way back through all the others. The cure is to blur the ending: money held back until later, a period for fixing faults, a maintenance arrangement, the chance of the next job. Then neither side can point at a stage and say “after this, nothing.”
Why does the course insist that a device which leaves the outcome beyond your control needs a delicate balance? Say it in your own words, and name the thing that is certain to happen eventually.
Something like: “the device has to be automatic enough that the other side really is deterred. But every automatic device gets a false alarm sooner or later, and the more automatic it is, the worse that false alarm is.”

The certain thing is the false alarm. The slides treat it as something that will happen, not as a risk you can design away. That is why the balance is delicate rather than solvable. The doomsday machine deters perfectly and fails terribly, and those are the same property.
A supplier has a firm rule: no credit, payment on delivery. A regular customer asks to pay two days late “just this once”. Then a week. Then he asks for thirty days on one small invoice. Then on all of them.

Name what the customer is doing, and say what the supplier should have done at the first step.
Salami tactics. Every request is small enough that saying no looks petty and risks a good customer, so the supplier gives in. Each thing he gives becomes the new starting line for the next request.

What to do at the first step: refuse the very first slice, because it is the only one that is cheap to refuse. Or take the decision out of your own hands. A written credit policy run by the accounts department (a contract plus mandated agents) makes the answer “I'm not allowed to” instead of “I'd rather not”.

Notice how this matches the whole day. The supplier's problem is again too much freedom. He is able to say yes, so he will be asked.
