Mixed Strategies I: making your opponent indifferent

The professor says out loud that you may need this calculation in the exam. About 21% of the marks live here.

⏸ Guess first

You're taking a penalty kick. You know your right-footed shot is stronger: you score more often with it. Should you take every penalty to the right?

Take 20 seconds. Commit to an answer before you read on.

🎧 Why you mix at all ~1 min

A quick map of what you'll learn today. Each section below also has its own short audio.

  • Zero-sum game: whatever one player gains, the other loses. It is written with one number per cell: player 1's payoff. Player 1 wants it high, player 2 wants it low.
  • Mixed strategy: you choose at random, with fixed proportions. The opposite is a pure strategy: you always pick the same action.
  • The indifference rule: your best mixture is the one that leaves your opponent indifferent. Indifferent means both of his choices pay him the same on average. He has no reason to prefer either one, so he has nothing to use against you.
  • Nash equilibrium: a pair of choices where neither player wants to change. Value of the game: the payoff that comes out of that equilibrium. In a zero-sum game the value is the same number in every Nash equilibrium.

One line to hold onto: you don't mix to win. You mix so you can't be read.

Zero-sum, and why one number is enough

🎧 Listen to this section

Some games are pure conflict. There is a fixed pie. Every crumb you take is a crumb the other person loses. Poker between two players. A penalty kick. Rock-paper-scissors. The course calls these zero-sum games.

The two payoffs always add to zero, so writing both is a waste of ink. The course uses a shorthand instead: one number per cell, and that number is player 1's payoff. Player 2's payoff is that same number with a minus sign.

Every zero-sum question on every past exam uses this shorthand. If you see a matrix with a single number in each cell, this is what you're looking at.

Player 1 (the rows) wants the number high. Player 2 (the columns) wants the same number low. One number, two opposite goals.

The two forms, side by side

Vizzini's battle of wits from The Princess Bride. Westley secretly poisons one goblet. Vizzini then picks a goblet to drink. Vizzini wants to guess right. Westley wants him to guess wrong. Here is the game written the long way:

The second number never told you anything new. It was always the first one with a minus sign.

Now look for a stable pair of choices. There isn't one. Whatever cell you point at, one of the two players wants to move. That's the "endless circle of logic" Vizzini talks himself into. The only way out is to stop being predictable.

You need randomness exactly when it hurts you if your opponent sees your choice before he answers.

The best mixture makes your opponent indifferent

🎧 Listen to this section

So you must mix. But in what proportions? Half and half? That feels fair, and it is usually wrong.

Here is the idea. Suppose your mixture pays your opponent more when he goes left than when he goes right. Then he will always go left. You have handed him a free advantage, and he keeps it for the rest of the game. The only mixture he cannot use against you is the one where he gets exactly the same average payoff whichever action he picks. He is then indifferent. He has no reason to prefer anything, and no way to read you.

You find your own mixture by making the opponent indifferent. Set his two expected payoffs (his two average payoffs) equal to each other. Then solve for your proportions.

The exam's data

The penalty-kick game from Presentation 5. Each number is the kicker's chance of scoring, in percent. The kicker picks the row and wants it high. The goalie picks the column and wants it low. Note that the right side is the stronger side for this kicker.

Goalie dives LeftGoalie dives Right
Kicker goes Left  (x)5895
Kicker goes Right  (1−x)9370

First check that no pair of pure strategies is stable. Whatever cell you pick, one player wants to move. So mixing is needed.

Two straight lines: the kicker's scoring chance against each of the goalie's two choices. Anywhere except the crossing, one line sits below the other. The goalie just picks that one. Only where they meet does the goalie's choice stop mattering.

Worked example

Use the penalty-kick table above. What proportion x of his kicks should the kicker send to the left? And what scoring rate does that guarantee him?
1
Write the kicker's payoff if the goalie dives Left.
He scores 58 when he kicks left, which is a share x of the time. He scores 93 when he kicks right, a share 1−x of the time.
58x + 93(1 − x) = 93 − 35x
2
Write the kicker's payoff if the goalie dives Right.
Same idea, other column.
95x + 70(1 − x) = 70 + 25x
3
Set them equal: this is the indifference condition.
Now the goalie's choice makes no difference to the kicker's average. In a zero-sum game that is the same as making the goalie indifferent, because his payoff is just minus the kicker's.
93 − 35x = 70 + 25x  →  23 = 60x
4
Solve, then put x back into either line to get the value.
x = 23/60 = 38.3%
93 − 35(0.383) = 79.6
✓ The kicker goes left 38.3% of the time. That guarantees him a 79.6% scoring rate, which is the value of the game. (Doing the same work on the goalie's side gives 41.7% left.)
Notice what just happened. The kicker's left foot is his weaker side: 58 against 93 when the goalie dives left. And he still uses it more than a third of the time. Not because it is good, but because never using it would make him easy to read.
Suppose the goalie trains hard and gets better at defending his right. The bottom-right number falls from 70 to 60. The kicker now scores less often when they both go right. Everything else stays the same.

Will the goalie now dive right more often, since he's better at it?
No, the opposite. The goalie dives left more often, going from 41.7% to 50%.

Why: getting better at the right side makes the kicker avoid the right. The kicker shifts left, from 38.3% up to 47.1%. The goalie follows him there. The value of the game drops from 79.6 to 76.5, so the goalie is really better off. He just gets there by using his improved side less.

The slides put it in one line: becoming better at something may make you use it less often. This is the surprising result of the whole lecture. It is exactly the kind of thing a concept question likes to ask.
One exception: when you should NOT mix. Mixing protects you from being read. But it cannot punish your opponent's mistake. So if you know your opponent has a habit (say, this kicker goes left too often), drop the mix. Just dive left, every time.

The risk: a clever opponent can fake a habit when the stakes are low, then switch when they are high. On the exam, the default is still to mix. Exploit only when the question tells you about a real, known bias.

Careful: not every zero-sum game needs mixing

🎧 Listen to this section

Here is the trap. Once you know the indifference method, it is tempting to see the words "zero-sum" and start writing equations at once. Sometimes there is nothing to solve.

Take the same penalty story, but now the kicker's left foot is really bad. Not just weaker: bad. One number changes the whole game:

Goalie dives LeftGoalie dives Right
Kicker goes Left3865
Kicker goes Right9370  ✓
Kicking right beats kicking left in both columns: 93 > 38 and 70 > 65. So the kicker always kicks right. Knowing that, the goalie picks the smaller number in that row: 70. The green cell is a stable pair, and no mixing is needed.

The test is quick. Look at the highlighted number: it is the smallest in its row and the largest in its column. When such a cell exists, that cell is the equilibrium and the value of the game is just that number. When no such cell exists, as in the 58/95/93/70 version, you must mix.

Before you write a single equation, look for a dominant strategy: a row that beats the other row in every column. If you find one, the game is already solved.
A zero-sum game, one number per cell = player 1's payoff:

LeftRight
Top47
Down25
Does this game need a mixed strategy? What is player 1's payoff in equilibrium?
No mixing. Player 1 gets 4.

Top beats Down in both columns (4 > 2 and 7 > 5), so player 1 always plays Top. Player 2 wants the number low, so facing Top she picks the smaller one: 4 rather than 7, which is Left.

Check the cell: 4 is the smallest in its row and the largest in its column. Stable. The equilibrium is (Top, Left) and the value is 4.
