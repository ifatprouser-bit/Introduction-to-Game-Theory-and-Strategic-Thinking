Repeated games, and sustaining cooperation

P3

One question, on every single paper, worth 7 points. It is in words, not numbers. One trap catches almost everybody. A short day: about 30 minutes.

⏸ Guess first

You and a rival will set prices for 8 months, then the market closes for good. Your rival says: “I will keep prices high, and I will forgive you once. The second time you undercut me, I go to war for the rest of the game.”

You want the biggest total profit. How many months should you undercut, and which ones?

Take 20 seconds. Say your answer out loud before you read on.

🎧 Where an opponent’s rule leaves a gap ~1 min

A quick map of what you'll learn today. Each section below also has its own short audio.

  • Finitely repeated game: the same game played a known number of times against the same person. In a prisoner's dilemma each of you picks cooperate (good for both of you) or defect (better for you alone, worse for both). Here the one backward-induction answer is defect in every round. Backward induction means you solve the last round first, then step back one round at a time.
  • Tit-for-tat: cooperate in round 1, then copy whatever your opponent did last round. Every paper offers it as an answer. It is almost never the best reply.
  • Grim trigger: cooperate until the opponent defects once, then defect forever.
  • Best reply: the plan that earns you the most, given what the opponent is doing. This is the exam question. You get the opponent's rule, and you are asked what you should do. Work backwards. Take every defection the rule leaves unpunished.
  • Sustaining cooperation: detection, punishment that is severe but not too severe, clear boundaries, and a low enough interest rate.

One line to hold onto: a defection is free whenever it cannot change what the opponent does next. Count the free ones. There is usually more than one.

The last round poisons the whole game P3

🎧 Listen to this section

A prisoner's dilemma played once has an obvious answer: defect. Both players do it, and both end up worse off than if they had cooperated. That is Day 2's material.

Now play it ten times with the same person, and both of you know it is ten. Cooperation starts to look possible. I behave, you behave, and we both collect the good payoff round after round. If you cheat me, I punish you next round. The future is what keeps us honest.

But the future runs out. Look at round 10. There is no round 11, so there is no punishment left to fear. In the last round, defecting is simply better. Both of us know it.

So round 10 is already spoiled. Now step back to round 9. I am deciding whether to behave. But I already know round 10 will be mutual defection whatever I do. So behaving in round 9 buys me nothing. I defect in round 9 too. Round 9 is now spoiled, and the same argument moves back to round 8.

Round 10 is dark because that is where the argument starts. Each step backwards spoils one more round, until every round says defect.
In a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma with a known end, the unique backward-induction equilibrium is to defect in every single round. Learn that sentence. It has been asked word for word.

In real experiments, pairs playing a 10-round prisoner's dilemma cooperate for most of the game. They only start defecting in the last few rounds. The theory says they should defect from round 1. Presentation 3 says their real behaviour can still be rational. Why?
Because a few players really do pay you back in kind. Suppose about 5% of players genuinely play tit-for-tat all the way to the end. Then cooperating early is not foolish. It is an investment in looking like one of them. If your opponent thinks you might be one of those players, they keep cooperating with you, and you keep collecting the good payoff.

So the selfish plan is still this. Cooperate early. Play tit-for-tat through the middle. Then start defecting in the last couple of rounds, when the disguise is worth nothing. (Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts & Wilson, 1982.)

That is the shape of every exam question on this topic. Your job is to find the exact round where the disguise stops paying.

Find the gap in the opponent's rule P3

🎧 Listen to this section

This is the whole exam question, and it is the same every year. The paper describes the opponent's rule in words. It tells you the game runs a fixed number of rounds. It offers five possible replies, and you pick the best one. There is no matrix to solve.

Here is the method. Go round by round, from the last round backwards. In each round, ask one question:

If I defect in this round, does it change anything the opponent does later?
If no → the defection is free. Take it.

The last round is always free, because there is no later round. Everybody gets that part. The mark is won or lost on the other free defections. There is nearly always at least one more.

Shape 1: the opponent who forgives

Take the mail-order pricing game above, played for 8 rounds. Your rival's rule: “I keep charging $80, and I forgive one undercut. Once you have undercut me twice, I charge $70 for the rest of the game.”

Her punishment only starts after your second defection. So your first defection costs you nothing at all. And a defection in round 8 costs you nothing either, because no round follows it. Count them: that is two free defections, not one.

Shape 2: the opponent who turns at a fixed round

Same game, 8 rounds. New rival rule: “I charge $80 in round 1. In rounds 2–6 I copy whatever you did last round. In rounds 7 and 8 I charge $70 no matter what.”

She turns in round 7. So rounds 7 and 8 are lost to you whatever you do. Now the key step. Her move in round 7 is already fixed. Your round-6 move cannot change it. So behaving in round 6 buys you nothing. Defect in round 6, not 7. That is one round earlier than she turns.

The two traps, side by side. Both are off-by-one errors, and they point in opposite directions.
• Forgiving opponent: you get a free defection at the start as well as at the end. “Cooperate all the way, then defect only in the last round” looks right. It is one defection short.
• Opponent who turns at round k: start defecting at round k−1, one round before she turns. One official solution says it outright: if you wait until she turns, you waste the round before it.
• “Play tit-for-tat” is offered as an option on the older papers. It has never once been the answer.

Worked example

The 8-round pricing game from Shape 1 above. Your rival forgives one undercut. Once you have undercut her twice, she charges $70 for the rest of the game. Here are five possible replies. Which earns the most, and what does each earn?
(i) charge $80 every round  ·  (ii) charge $80 in rounds 1–7 and $70 in round 8  ·  (iii) charge $70 every round  ·  (iv) play tit-for-tat  ·  (v) charge $70 in rounds 1 and 8, $80 in between.
1
(i) Always $80.
She always charges $80 too. Eight rounds of mutual cooperation.
8 × 75 = 600
2
(ii) $80 in 1–7, $70 in round 8.
Seven rounds of 75, then one undercut against a co-operator. This is the tempting wrong answer.
7 × 75 + 120 = 645
3
(iii) Always $70.
She forgives round 1, so she still charges $80 in round 2. After your second undercut she goes to war from round 3 on.
120 + 120 + 6 × 70 = 660
4
(iv) Tit-for-tat.
You cooperate in round 1. She cooperated, so you cooperate in round 2, and so on to the end. You never defect at all, so this is the same as (i).
8 × 75 = 600
5
(v) $70 in rounds 1 and 8, $80 in between.
Two undercuts against a co-operator, six rounds of mutual $80, zero rounds of war.
120 + 6 × 75 + 120 = 690
✓ (v) 690 > (iii) 660 > (ii) 645 > (i) = (iv) 600. Look where tit-for-tat lands: joint last, tied with never defecting at all. That is exactly what it does here. And the “obvious” answer (ii) is beaten even by always undercutting.
Round 1 is not special, and the exam knows it. What matters is that your second undercut lands in round 8. Then the punishment would fall in a round that does not exist. The first undercut can sit anywhere in rounds 1–7. So this game has seven equally good replies, all scoring 690: (1,8), (2,8), (3,8) … (7,8).

The paper still has to print one of them, so it prints the tidiest: defect first and last. Do not read that as “the answer is always first and last”. Read it as “take every free defection the rule leaves you”. A rule that forgives nothing leaves only one.
Same 8-round pricing game. New rival rule: “I charge $80 in round 1. In rounds 2–7 I copy whatever you did in the previous round. In round 8 I charge $70 whatever happens.”

Which rounds should you undercut in, and what is your total?
Undercut in rounds 7 and 8. Total 640.

Walk it backwards, one round at a time.

Round 8 is free. She charges $70 no matter what, and no round follows it.

Round 7 is free too. She is copying your round-6 move. You were good in round 6, so she charges $80 here. Now ask what an undercut costs you. She would copy it and charge $70 in round 8. But she was already going to charge $70 in round 8. So it costs you nothing.

Round 6 is not free. Undercut here and she charges $70 in round 7. You lose the 120 you were going to collect there. Stop.

Now add it up. Rounds 1–6 at 75 = 450. Round 7: you $70 against her $80 = 120. Round 8: both $70 = 70. Total 450 + 120 + 70 = 640.

Compare two wrong plans. Cooperate through round 7 and undercut only in round 8: 7 × 75 + 70 = 595. You would have thrown away 45. Undercut from round 6 instead: 635. Going too early costs you as well.

Notice the pattern. She turns in round 8. You start in round 7, one round earlier. In Shape 2 she turned in round 7, so you started in round 6. Same rule every time: start one round before she does.
Why is “play tit-for-tat” wrong as a best reply, even though tit-for-tat is famous for being a strong strategy?
Because it only reacts. Tit-for-tat never defects first. Against an opponent who cooperates, tit-for-tat cooperates all the way to the end. So it never takes the free defection in the last round, and it never takes a forgiven early one either.

Against the forgiving rival above it scores 600: joint worst of the five options, tied with never defecting at all. That is exactly what it does.

Against a rival who turns at a fixed round it is worse still. In the Shape 2 game it scores 540 against the best reply's 635. It keeps cooperating right up to the round she turns. It walks into the sucker payoff of 20, and only then copies her. It is always one round late, which is exactly the mistake this whole section is about.

Tit-for-tat is a good strategy for sustaining cooperation when both sides are choosing freely. It is a bad best reply when the opponent's rule is already fixed and you can see the gaps in it.

Why cooperation survives anyway P3

Theory says defect in every round. Yet cartels hold for years, and neighbours share a driveway for decades. Presentation 3 names four things that make cooperation stick.

The discounting, in one picture

Two quantities. The prize for cheating is a single lump sum you collect today. The cost of cheating is a small loss that repeats every round, forever. What that stream is worth today depends on the interest rate.

A payment that repeats every year forever is called a perpetuity. A perpetuity of x a year, starting next year, is worth x / r today. That is the only formula on this page.

The green line never moves, because the prize for cheating is the same whatever the interest rate. The purple curve falls as r rises, because a far-off stream is worth less when money is expensive. Where they cross is the tipping point.

Worked example

The two mail-order firms play the pricing game every year, forever, with no end date. Payoffs as before: both at $80 gives 75 each per year; undercutting a co-operator gives you 120 that year; both at $70 gives 70 each. Your rival is a conditional co-operator. She holds $80 as long as you do, and she switches to $70 forever if you ever undercut her.

Above what annual interest rate r does undercutting become your best reply?
1
What do you gain, and when?
Undercutting today turns this year's 75 into 120. That is a one-off gain, collected now.
immediate gain = 120 − 75 = 45
2
What do you lose, and when?
From next year on she charges $70 forever, so every future year gives you 70 instead of 75.
loss = 75 − 70 = 5, every year, starting next year
3
Put the future stream into today's money.
A payment of x every year forever, starting next year, is worth x/r today.
value today of the losses = 5 / r
4
Set the two equal and solve for the tipping point.
45 = 5 / r  →  45r = 5  →  r = 5/45 = 1/9
1/9 = 0.1111… = 11.1%
✓ r = 1/9 ≈ 11.1%. Below that rate the future stream is worth more than the lump sum. So always cooperating is the best reply. Above it, take the 45 and defect forever. Check it: at 5% the stream is worth 5/0.05 = 100, well above 45. At 20% it is worth only 25, below 45.
A different pair of firms. Holding the agreed price gives each 100 per year; undercutting a co-operator gives 130 that year; if both cut, each gets 90 per year. Same conditional co-operator, and again the game never ends.

What is the highest interest rate at which cooperation is still your best reply?
r = 1/3 ≈ 33.3%.

Immediate gain: 130 − 100 = 30. Loss every future year: 100 − 90 = 10. Value of that stream today: 10/r.

Set them equal: 30 = 10/r → r = 10/30 = 1/3 = 33.3%.

Cooperation survives at much higher interest rates here than for the mail-order firms, because the punishment bites harder next to the prize. The general rule is r* = (R − P) / (T − R). Here T is the temptation, R is the reward when both cooperate, and P is the punishment. A big punishment and a small temptation make cooperation easy to sustain.
The New England groundfish fishery collapsed species by species over a century. Every boat knew that if everyone kept fishing hard the stock would die. Which of the four conditions above failed hardest, and why?
Detection and punishment failed, because there were too many players.

With two firms, each can see the other's price, and each can make a believable threat of a price war. With hundreds of boats on an open ocean, you cannot see who over-fished. Even if you could, no single boat can punish another. The threat cannot be carried out, so it is not a threat.

This is the multi-player prisoner's dilemma from Presentation 3, the “tragedy of the commons”. Global warming has the same shape. It is also why the public-good experiments only keep contributions up when players get a costly punishment option. Without it, contributions fall to almost nothing.

In the lab: which dilemma gets more cooperation? P3

Real people cooperate more than theory says. A big study of many lab experiments (Mengel, 2018) found what moves them. The professor taught it in class and put it in a homework question.

FactorPlain wordsEffect
1. WasteHow much both lose if both defect. Measure: (R − P) / RBig waste → more cooperation
2. RiskHow much worse it is to cooperate alone (S) than to defect too (P)Big risk → less cooperation
3. TemptationHow much you gain by cheating (T − R)Tiny effect only
R = both cooperate · P = both defect · S = you cooperate alone (the “sucker”) · T = you cheat alone.
People fear two bad outcomes: the waste of mutual defection, and being the lone sucker. More fear of waste → they cooperate. More fear of being the sucker → they defect.

Waste: in A, mutual defection loses (60 − 55)/60 ≈ 8%. In B it loses (60 − 15)/60 = 75%. Big waste in B → more cooperation.

Risk: in A, cooperating alone drops you from 55 to 10. Very risky. In B, from 15 to 12. Almost no risk. Low risk in B → more cooperation.

Temptation is the same in both (90 − 60 = 30), so it does not decide anything.

Watch out: if the two factors point in opposite directions, you cannot say which game wins. The professor pointed this out when he went over the homework.
