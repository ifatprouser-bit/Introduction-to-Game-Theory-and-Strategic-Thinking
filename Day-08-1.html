Repeated games, and sustaining cooperation

P3

One question on every paper, worth 7 points. Words, not numbers. One trap catches almost everybody. About 30 minutes.

⏸ Guess first

You and a rival will set prices for 8 months, then the market closes for good. Your rival says: “I will keep prices high, and I will forgive you once. The second time you undercut me, I go to war for the rest of the game.”

You want the biggest total profit. How many months should you undercut, and which ones?

Take 20 seconds. Say your answer out loud before you read on.

🎧 Where an opponent’s rule leaves a gap ~1 min

A quick map of what you'll learn today. Each section below also has its own short audio.

  • Prisoner's dilemma: each player picks cooperate (good for both) or defect (better for you alone, worse for both).
  • Finitely repeated game: the same game, a known number of rounds, same opponent.
  • Backward induction: solve the last round first, then step back one round at a time.
  • Tit-for-tat: cooperate in round 1, then copy the opponent's last move.
  • Grim trigger: cooperate until the opponent defects once, then defect forever.
  • Best reply: the plan that earns you the most against the opponent's rule. This is the exam question.

One line to hold onto: a defection is free whenever it cannot change what the opponent does next.

The last round poisons the whole game P3

🎧 Listen to this section

Played once, a prisoner's dilemma ends in mutual defection (Day 2). Now play it ten times, same person, both know it is ten:

  1. Round 10: no round 11, so no punishment to fear. Defecting is simply better.
  2. Round 9: round 10 is defection whatever I do. Behaving now buys nothing. Defect.
  3. Round 8, 7, … 1: the same argument, one step back each time.
The reasoning starts at round 10 and spoils one round per step, back to round 1.
In a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma with a known end, the unique backward-induction equilibrium is to defect in every single round. Learn that sentence. It has been asked word for word.

Worked example

Two mail-order clothing firms play the pricing game from Presentation 3 for exactly three seasons, and both know it is three. Each season they simultaneously charge $80 (“cooperate”) or $70 (“defect”). Profits per season, in thousands, first number = Firm 1:

In real experiments, pairs playing a 10-round prisoner's dilemma cooperate for most of the game. They only start defecting in the last few rounds. The theory says they should defect from round 1. Presentation 3 says their real behaviour can still be rational. Why?
Because a few players really do pay you back in kind.
  • Suppose about 5% of players play tit-for-tat all the way to the end
  • Then cooperating early is an investment in looking like one of them. Your opponent keeps cooperating, and you keep the good payoff
  • The selfish plan: cooperate early, tit-for-tat through the middle, defect in the last couple of rounds, when the disguise is worth nothing (Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts & Wilson, 1982)
Every exam question on this topic asks you to find the exact round where the disguise stops paying.

Find the gap in the opponent's rule P3

🎧 Listen to this section

The exam question, every year:

  • The opponent's rule, in words
  • A fixed number of rounds
  • Five possible replies. Pick the best one
  • No matrix to solve
The method:
  1. Start at the last round and go backwards.
  2. In each round ask: if I defect here, does it change anything the opponent does later?
  3. No → the defection is free. Take it. Yes → stop.

The last round is always free. The mark is won on the other free defection. There is nearly always one more.

Shape 1: the opponent who forgives

The mail-order game, 8 rounds. Rival's rule: “I keep charging $80, and I forgive one undercut. Once you have undercut me twice, I charge $70 for the rest of the game.”

  • Your first undercut: forgiven, so free
  • Round 8: nothing follows, so free
  • That is two free defections

Both traps are off by one round, in opposite directions.
  • Forgiving opponent: a free defection at the start too. “Defect only in the last round” is one defection short.
  • Opponent who turns at round k: start at round k−1. One official solution: if you wait until she turns, you waste the round before it.
  • “Play tit-for-tat” appears on older papers. It has never once been the answer.

Worked example

The 8-round pricing game from Shape 1 above. Your rival forgives one undercut. Once you have undercut her twice, she charges $70 for the rest of the game. Here are five possible replies. Which earns the most, and what does each earn?
(i) charge $80 every round  ·  (ii) charge $80 in rounds 1–7 and $70 in round 8  ·  (iii) charge $70 every round  ·  (iv) play tit-for-tat  ·  (v) charge $70 in rounds 1 and 8, $80 in between.
1
(i) Always $80.
She charges $80 too, all eight rounds.
8 × 75 = 600
2
(ii) $80 in 1–7, $70 in round 8.
One undercut, at the end. The tempting wrong answer.
7 × 75 + 120 = 645
3
(iii) Always $70.
Round 1 forgiven, so she charges $80 in round 2. After your second undercut, war from round 3.
120 + 120 + 6 × 70 = 660
4
(iv) Tit-for-tat.
She never defects, so you never defect. Same as (i).
8 × 75 = 600
5
(v) $70 in rounds 1 and 8, $80 in between.
Two undercuts, six rounds of mutual $80, no war.
120 + 6 × 75 + 120 = 690
✓ (v) 690 > (iii) 660 > (ii) 645 > (i) = (iv) 600. The “obvious” (ii) loses even to always undercutting.
Round 1 is not special.
  • What matters: your second undercut lands in round 8
  • The first can sit anywhere in rounds 1–7, so seven replies score 690: (1,8), (2,8), (3,8) … (7,8)
  • The paper prints the tidiest one: first and last
  • So don't learn “first and last”. Learn “take every free defection the rule leaves you”. A rule that forgives nothing leaves only one
Same 8-round pricing game. New rival rule: “I charge $80 in round 1. In rounds 2–7 I copy whatever you did in the previous round. In round 8 I charge $70 whatever happens.”

Which rounds should you undercut in, and what is your total?
Undercut in rounds 7 and 8. Total 640. Walk it backwards:
  1. Round 8 is free. She charges $70 no matter what, and no round follows.
  2. Round 7 is free too. She copies your round 6, so she charges $80 here. Your undercut would make her charge $70 in round 8, which she does anyway. Cost: nothing.
  3. Round 6 is not free. Undercut here and she charges $70 in round 7: you lose the 120 there. Stop.
Total: rounds 1–6 at 75 = 450, round 7 = 120, round 8 = 70. 450 + 120 + 70 = 640.
  • Undercut only in round 8: 7 × 75 + 70 = 595. You throw away 45
  • Undercut from round 6: 635. Too early costs you too
The pattern: she turns in round 8, you start in round 7. In Shape 2 she turned in 7, you started in 6. Start one round before she does.
Why is “play tit-for-tat” wrong as a best reply, even though tit-for-tat is famous for being a strong strategy?
Because it only reacts. It never defects first.
  • Forgiving rival: it cooperates to the end and takes no free defection. 600: joint worst of the five, tied with never defecting
  • Rival who turns at a fixed round (Shape 2): 540 against the best reply's 635. It cooperates into the round she turns, takes the sucker payoff of 20 (you cooperate while she defects), and only then copies her. Always one round late
Tit-for-tat is good for sustaining cooperation when both sides choose freely. It is a bad best reply to a fixed rule whose gaps you can see.

Most likely to be tested, and it feels wrong: punishment must not be too severe. Honest mistakes happen. A threat that ends the relationship over one late delivery kills the relationship.

The discounting, in one picture

  • Prize for cheating: one lump sum, today
  • Cost of cheating: a small loss every round, forever
  • A payment of x every year forever, starting next year (a perpetuity), is worth x / r today. The only formula on this page
  • Green line: the prize for cheating. Fixed, whatever r is
  • Purple curve: the lost stream. Falls as r rises
  • The crossing is the tipping point

Worked example

The two mail-order firms play the pricing game every year, forever, with no end date. Payoffs as before: both at $80 gives 75 each per year; undercutting a co-operator gives you 120 that year; both at $70 gives 70 each. Your rival is a conditional co-operator. She holds $80 as long as you do, and she switches to $70 forever if you ever undercut her.

Above what annual interest rate r does undercutting become your best reply?
1
What do you gain, and when?
This year's 75 becomes 120. Once, now.
immediate gain = 120 − 75 = 45
2
What do you lose, and when?
She charges $70 forever: 70 instead of 75 every future year.
loss = 75 − 70 = 5, every year, starting next year
3
Put the future stream into today's money.
value today of the losses = 5 / r
4
Set the two equal and solve for the tipping point.
45 = 5 / r  →  45r = 5  →  r = 5/45 = 1/9
1/9 = 0.1111… = 11.1%
✓ r = 1/9 ≈ 11.1%.
  • Below it: the stream is worth more. Always cooperating is the best reply
  • Above it: take the 45 and defect forever
  • Check: at 5% the stream is worth 5/0.05 = 100 (above 45). At 20% only 25 (below 45)
A different pair of firms. Holding the agreed price gives each 100 per year; undercutting a co-operator gives 130 that year; if both cut, each gets 90 per year. Same conditional co-operator, and again the game never ends.

What is the highest interest rate at which cooperation is still your best reply?
r = 1/3 ≈ 33.3%.
  1. Immediate gain: 130 − 100 = 30
  2. Loss every future year: 100 − 90 = 10, worth 10/r today
  3. 30 = 10/r → r = 10/30 = 1/3 = 33.3%
The punishment bites harder next to the prize, so cooperation survives higher rates. General rule: r* = (R − P) / (T − R), with T = temptation, R = reward when both cooperate, P = punishment. Big punishment, small temptation → cooperation is easy to sustain.
The New England groundfish fishery collapsed species by species over a century. Every boat knew that if everyone kept fishing hard the stock would die. Which of the four conditions above failed hardest, and why?
Detection and punishment failed: too many players.
  • Two firms can see each other's price and threaten a price war
  • Hundreds of boats on an open ocean: you cannot see who over-fished, and no single boat can punish another. A threat you cannot carry out is not a threat
This is the multi-player prisoner's dilemma from Presentation 3, the “tragedy of the commons” (global warming has the same shape). Public-good experiments keep contributions up only with a costly punishment option. Without it, contributions fall to almost nothing.

In the lab: which dilemma gets more cooperation? P3

A big study of many lab experiments (Mengel, 2018) found what moves real people. The professor taught it and put it in a homework question.

FactorPlain wordsEffect
1. WasteHow much both lose if both defect. Measure: (R − P) / RBig waste → more cooperation
2. RiskHow much worse it is to cooperate alone (S) than to defect too (P)Big risk → less cooperation
3. TemptationHow much you gain by cheating (T − R)Tiny effect only
R = both cooperate · P = both defect · S = you cooperate alone (the “sucker”) · T = you cheat alone.
People fear two bad outcomes: the waste of mutual defection, and being the lone sucker. More fear of waste → they cooperate. More fear of being the sucker → they defect.

In which game would you expect more cooperation in the lab? Check both factors before you answer.
Game B. Both factors point the same way.
  • Waste: A loses (60 − 55)/60 ≈ 8%. B loses (60 − 15)/60 = 75%. Big waste in B → more cooperation
  • Risk: cooperating alone drops you 55 → 10 in A (very risky), 15 → 12 in B (almost none). Low risk in B → more cooperation
  • Temptation: the same in both (90 − 60 = 30), so it decides nothing
Watch out: if the two factors point in opposite directions, you cannot say which game wins. The professor said so when he went over the homework.
