P1 Say the two definitions 5 min

Two sentences, out loud, before you look at anything. What is a strategy? What is a dominant strategy?

Strategy: a rule that names your move in every case that could come up, not the one move you happen to make.  Dominant strategy: a strategy that gives you a higher payoff than every other choice, whatever the other player does.

The word "every" in the first definition is what the exam tests. A strategy is written before the game starts, so it must survive any surprise. Look at the shape below. Player 2 has two places where he might have to move. His plan has to cover both, even the one he never reaches.

Player 1 moves once. Player 2 might move after Left or after Right. He will only ever reach one of them. His strategy must still say what to do at both.
How many strategies does player 2 have in this tree? And how many does player 1 have?
Player 2 has four. Player 1 has two.

Player 2's plan must fill in two blanks (what to do after Left, and what to do after Right) with two options each. That is 2 × 2 = four complete plans: Up / Up, Up / Down, Down / Up, Down / Down (first word: after Left; second word: after Right).

Player 1 has only one place to move, so he has just two strategies: Left or Right.

Why the exam cares. "Player 2 plays Down" is not a strategy: it does not say what he does after Right. If you can say that sentence, you have the definition. Now say the second one: a dominant strategy beats every alternative in every column, not on average, and not usually.

P2 Solve one tree, last node first 5 min

Same shape, now with payoffs. First number in each ending is player 1's, second is player 2's. Cover the reveal and solve it.

The green path is the answer. Check it only after you have found it yourself.
What does player 1 get in the backward-induction equilibrium?
Player 1 gets 4.

His turn after Left, first. Player 2 reads only his own number: 2 if he plays Up, 5 if he plays Down. He plays Down, so the Left branch is worth (4, 5).

His turn after Right, next. 3 if he plays Up, 8 if he plays Down. He plays Down, so the Right branch is worth (2, 8).

Now the root. Player 1 compares the two branch values, reading only his own number: Left is worth 4, Right is worth 2. He goes Left. Final outcome (4, 5).

Both traps are here. 7 is the biggest payoff player 1 can see in the tree, and he can never reach it. Player 2 would have to hand it over, and he will not. The other trap is 6. It sits on the branch player 1 really does choose, so it is the most tempting wrong answer on the page.

P3 Name one dilemma, then one best reply 5 min

Two farmers take water from the same well. Each one chooses to pump normally or to pump hard. First number is farmer 1's harvest, second is farmer 2's.

Farmer 2 pumps normallyFarmer 2 pumps hard
Farmer 1 pumps normally9,  92,  12
Farmer 1 pumps hard12,  25,  5
How to read this table. Rows: farmer 1's choice. Columns: farmer 2's choice. In each cell, farmer 1's harvest comes first and farmer 2's second.
The two-part test: each player has a dominant strategy, and the cell they both land on is worse for both than some other cell.
(a) Is this a prisoner's dilemma? Show both halves of the test.

(b) Now the same game runs for ten seasons. Your neighbour's rule: pump normally in season 1, and keep pumping normally as long as you have never pumped hard. The moment you pump hard, he pumps hard in every season that is left. What is your best reply?
(a) Yes. Dominance: for farmer 1, pumping hard beats pumping normally in both columns: 12 > 9 and 5 > 2. The grid is symmetric, so the same is true for farmer 2. Both pump hard. The second half: that lands them on (5, 5), and (9, 9) is better for both. Two dominant strategies lead somewhere both players dislike. That is the dilemma, and it is why the well runs dry.

(b) Pump normally in seasons 1–9, pump hard in season 10.

This neighbour never forgives: one hard season and cooperation is gone for good. So pumping hard early buys you 12 once and costs you the difference between 9 and 5 in every season that follows. Terrible. But the last season is free: there are no seasons left for him to punish you in, so you take the 12 and walk away. Nine seasons at 9 is 81, plus 12 makes 93. Never pumping hard at all gives 90. Pumping hard in every season gives just 57. Pumping hard in season 1 and then going back to normal is worst of all at 30, because you have triggered him and get nothing back for it.

Careful with this one. "Cooperate until the last round" is the right answer here, against an opponent who never forgives. It stops being right as soon as the opponent forgives a slip, because then round 1 is free too. Always read what the opponent forgives before you choose.

How to read this table. Rows: player 1's choice. Columns: player 2's choice. In each cell, player 1's payoff comes first and player 2's second.
Columns down for player 1, reading only the first number. Rows across for player 2, reading only the second. A cell marked twice is a pure Nash equilibrium: neither player can do better by moving alone.
How many pure Nash equilibria does this game have? And what is the highest payoff player 1 can get in one of them?
Two equilibria. Player 1's best is 8.

Player 1, column by column (first numbers only): Left is 4, 5, 7 → Down. Center is 6, 3, 6 → Up. Right is 2, 8, 5 → Middle.

Careful in Center: 6 appears twice, at Up and at Down. Both are best replies, so both get a mark.

Player 2, row by row (second numbers only): Up is 3, 5, 7 → Right. Middle is 3, 2, 4 → Right. Down is 1, 6, 5 → Center.

Cells with both marks: (Middle, Right) = (8, 4) and (Down, Center) = (6, 6). That is two, and player 1's highest equilibrium payoff is 8.

Notice that player 1's better equilibrium is player 2's worse one. Nothing says the two of you agree about which equilibrium is the good one.

P5 One mixed equilibrium, start to finish 5 min

🎧 Listen to this section

Zero-sum shorthand: one number per cell, and it is player 1's payoff. Player 1 wants it high, player 2 wants it low. Do the whole thing: both proportions and the value.

LeftRight
Top36
Bottom53
How to read this table. Rows: player 1's choice. Columns: player 2's choice. One number per cell: player 1's payoff. Player 2 gets minus that, so player 1 wants it high and player 2 wants it low.

Dominance check first: neither row beats the other in both columns (3 < 5 but 6 > 3). And neither column is better for player 2 in both rows. Saddle check: the best player 1 can guarantee himself is 3, and the worst player 2 can be held to is 5. Those two do not meet, so no single cell is stable. You must mix.

Find how often player 1 plays Top, how often player 2 plays Left, the value of the game, and the probability that the outcome is (Top, Left).
Top 40% of the time; Left 60% of the time; value 4.2; P(Top, Left) = 24%.

Player 1's mixture (call it p for Top). It must leave player 2 with nothing to choose between, so the two columns must come out level:
Left: 3p + 5(1−p) = 5 − 2p  Right: 6p + 3(1−p) = 3 + 3p
5 − 2p = 3 + 3p → 5p = 2 → p = 0.4.

Player 2's mixture (call it q for Left). It must leave player 1 with nothing to choose between, so the two rows must come out level:
Top: 3q + 6(1−q) = 6 − 3q  Bottom: 5q + 3(1−q) = 3 + 2q
6 − 3q = 3 + 2q → 5q = 3 → q = 0.6.

The value of the game is what player 1 gets on average when both mix like this. Put either mixture back in: 5 − 2(0.4) = 4.2. Check it the other way: 6 − 3(0.6) = 4.2. Same number. That agreement is your proof you did not slip.

The outcome cell: both players have to move, so multiply. 0.4 × 0.6 = 24%. Not 40% and not 60%: those are the two single proportions, and both will be on the option list. And not 16%, which is (Top, Right).

P6 Recite the eight devices 5 min

Cover the grid. List all eight out loud. Then uncover and see which ones you missed. The missing ones are almost always the last three.

1 · ContractsBind yourself in writing. Needs a third party to enforce it, and must survive renegotiation. NATO Article 5; the kitchen paid by stages.
2 · ReputationYou play many games. Carrying out a costly threat once buys belief later.
3 · Cutting off communicationMake yourself unreachable so you cannot be talked out of it. Costs you flexibility.
4 · Burning bridgesDestroy your own retreat so that fighting is your only option.
5 · Leaving the outcome beyond your controlHand the trigger to a machine or a rule. Strong enough to deter, not so strong that an accident is fatal.
6 · Moving in small stepsBreak one big risky promise into many small ones. Works only when there is no obvious final step.
7 · TeamworkPeer pressure and collective resolve: the group enforces it on you. Alcoholics Anonymous; the Roman army.
8 · Mandated negotiating agentsSend someone whose hands are publicly tied: a union leader with a restrictive mandate, an agent, a clerk who must follow the rule.
Name the device in each story. (i) A call-centre agent tells an angry customer that the cancellation fee is set by the system and nobody on the floor is allowed to waive it. (ii) A translator and a new client have never worked together and have signed nothing. The client sends one chapter at a time and pays on delivery, and there is always more of the book to come. (iii) Allied states sign a treaty stating that an armed attack on any one of them shall be treated as an attack on all. (iv) A recovering alcoholic joins a group where every week he must stand up in front of the others and say whether he drank.

And the bonus: what is salami tactics?
(i) Mandated negotiating agent. His hands are visibly tied by someone else, so arguing with him is pointless, and that is exactly the point. The slides put store clerks and officials who must follow fixed rules in this same box.

(ii) Moving in small steps. The three signals are all there: no trust between the two sides, nothing written down, and no clear final step. There is always more of the book. Each delivery is a small bet neither side minds losing.

(iii) Contracts. This is NATO Article 5, and Presentation 6 files it under device 1, not under teamwork. It is a written agreement enforced by a third party, and that is what makes it a contract. The slide lists it as the cold-war use of contracts, alongside the monopolist's beat-the-competition clause.

(iv) Teamwork. Peer pressure and collective resolve: the cost of breaking the commitment is your pride and standing in front of people you face every week. The lecture's other teamwork example is the Roman army, where deserting was punished by death, and so was failing to kill a deserter.

Do not mix (iii) and (iv) up. Teamwork is pressure from inside a group you belong to. A treaty between states is a contract, however military it sounds.

Salami tactics is the mirror image: using small steps to break someone else's commitment. Each slice is too thin to be worth a war, and after enough slices the whole thing is gone. The device and the counter-device are the same trick pointed in opposite directions.
The one real overlap. The professor uses the same kitchen-remodelling story for two different devices: under contracts ("contract with payment linked to progress in work") and again under moving in small steps ("lack of trust between homeowner and contractor, and an incomplete contract"). If a story like that appears, the test is whether an enforceable written agreement exists. Signed and enforced → contracts. No trust, nothing written, no obvious last step → small steps.
