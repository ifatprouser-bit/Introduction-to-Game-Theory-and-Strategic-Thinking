Name that game: the five game types side by side

P3P4

Your teacher splits these five games across two lectures. The exam puts them in one question and asks you to choose. Dominance and the prisoner's dilemma are about 13% of the paper. Counting equilibria on top of that is the biggest topic there is.

⏸ Guess first

Two friends want to spend the evening together. He would rather go bowling. She would rather see a film. Both would much rather be together than alone. So there are two sensible endings: both bowling, or both at the cinema.

Is one of those two endings better for both of them?

Take 20 seconds. Commit to an answer before you read on.

🎧 What tells the five games apart ~1 min

A quick map of what you'll learn today. Each section below also has its own short audio. Everything you need is printed on the page, so this day works fine on paper. Print it and the hidden answers come out with it.

  • Dominant strategy: a choice that gives you your best payoff no matter what the other player does. Strategic rule: if you have one, use it.
  • Dominated strategy: a choice that is always worse for you than some other choice of yours. Rule: never play it.
  • Pure Nash equilibrium: a cell where neither player wants to move. Counting these cells is the fastest way to sort a matrix.
  • Pareto-ranked: one outcome is better for both players than another. Not the same as "bigger total" and not the same as "better on average".

One line to hold onto: first ask who has a dominant strategy; then count the stable cells; then ask whether the two players agree about which one is nicer.

Dominance, and why a prisoner's dilemma needs two ticks P3

🎧 Listen to this section

A dominant strategy is a choice that gives you your best payoff whatever the other player does. You don't have to guess about him. You look down your own rows, and one of them wins in every column.

Its mirror is a dominated strategy: a choice that is always worse for you than some other choice of yours. Same logic, opposite sign.

If you have a dominant strategy, use it. If a strategy is dominated, never play it. You don't need to predict your opponent to obey either rule.

Albert Tucker's story from 1950. Two criminals are arrested and held apart. On the small charge each gets one year. If one testifies against the other, the talker walks free and his partner gets twenty years. If both testify, both get five. Each prisoner only wants to shorten his own sentence.

Testifying is dominant for each of them. It shortens your sentence whatever your partner does. So both testify and both serve five years, when staying quiet would have cost each of them one year.

Worked example

Two rival mail-order clothes firms each print $80 (cooperate) or $70 (defect) in their winter catalogue. Profits in thousands, firm 1 first: if both cooperate, 75 each. If firm 1 cooperates and firm 2 defects, 20 to firm 1 and 120 to firm 2. The other way round, 120 to firm 1 and 20 to firm 2. If both defect, 70 each.

Is this a prisoner's dilemma? Check both conditions, in order.
1
Does firm 1 have a dominant strategy?
Compare its two rows, column by column. If firm 2 cooperates: 120 beats 75. If firm 2 defects: 70 beats 20.
defect wins in both columns → dominant
2
Does firm 2 have a dominant strategy?
Same check across each row, reading the second number. If firm 1 cooperates: 120 beats 75. If firm 1 defects: 70 beats 20.
defect wins for firm 2 too → first condition holds
3
Where do the two dominant strategies land?
Bottom right. Both firms defect and each earns 70.
4
Now the second condition: is some other cell better for both?
Look at the top-left cell: 75 each.
75 > 70 for firm 1  AND  75 > 70 for firm 2
Both firms would rather be there. The condition holds.
✓ Yes, this is a prisoner's dilemma. Both conditions passed: every player has a dominant strategy, and the cell those strategies produce is worse for everybody than another cell. Miss the second check and you will call half the matrices on the exam a prisoner's dilemma.

The same shape, with a jackpot

The TV show Golden Balls ends with exactly this game for real money. Two contestants simultaneously choose split or steal. Split and split, they share. One steals, he takes everything. Both steal, both go home with nothing. For an average jackpot of $20,000, in thousands:

SplitSteal
Split10,  100,  20
Steal20,  00,  0

The course uses this as its high-stakes prisoner's dilemma, and the story is right. Personal incentive pulls both people to an outcome that is terrible for both. About half of contestants split anyway.

One honest detail worth seeing once. Look at the bottom row. Steal gives 20 against split, but only 0 against steal, which is the same as splitting would give. So stealing is weakly dominant, not strictly dominant. That tie means this matrix has three stable cells rather than one. No past-paper matrix does this. Every prisoner's-dilemma question in the five papers has strict inequalities, so the two-tick test works cleanly. Notice the tie, then move on.
Two neighbouring shops each decide whether to advertise. Payoffs (shop 1 first, in thousands):
AdvertiseStay quiet
Advertise5,  53,  2
Stay quiet2,  31,  1
Run both checks of the test on this matrix. Is it a prisoner's dilemma?
No. The first condition holds. Advertising beats staying quiet for shop 1 in both columns (5 > 2 and 3 > 1), and the same for shop 2. So both advertise and both get 5.

But now run the second check. Is there any cell where both shops do better than 5? Look: 3, 2, 1… nothing comes close. The dominant-strategy outcome is already the best cell in the matrix for both of them.

So this is a game with an obvious answer and a happy ending, not a dilemma. Dominance alone is never enough. That gap is exactly where the exam sets its trap.
Two suppliers feed the same assembly line, and each decides privately whether to deliver on time or quietly slip the shared deadline. Slipping saves you effort but delays the other one badly. Measured in weeks of delay: if both deliver, 2 weeks each. If one slips, the one who slipped waits 0 weeks and the one let down waits 12. If both slip, 6 weeks each.

Turn this into a payoff matrix yourself, then find the equilibrium and say which cell both suppliers would prefer.
First the sign. The story is told in delay, and more delay is worse. So each payoff is minus the weeks:
DeliverSlip
Deliver−2,  −2−12,  0
Slip0,  −12−6,  −6
Equilibrium: (slip, slip). Slipping is dominant for each supplier: 0 beats −2 in the left column, and −6 beats −12 in the right one.

And (deliver, deliver) gives −2 each, which beats −6 for both. Two conditions, two ticks: a prisoner's dilemma, with no prison in it.

The habit worth taking from this is the sign. When a story is told in costs (weeks late, money lost, years served), turn it into negative payoffs before you compare anything. If you do not, you will read every comparison backwards and find the wrong dominant strategy.

Two equilibria: the only question is how they are ranked P4

🎧 Listen to this section

Three of the five games have exactly two pure equilibria. So counting alone cannot separate them. You need one more question, and it is always the same one:

Two pure equilibria? Ask: is one of them better for both players?
Yes → stag hunt. No → battle of the sexes or chicken.

Here they are together. Green cells are the pure equilibria: the cells where neither player wants to move.

Single-lane bridge. Also not ranked, but the green cells have moved off the diagonal. One yields, one goes.

Read the three left to right and the pattern is clean. Stag hunt: the players agree which equilibrium they want, and coordinating is only a question of nerve. Battle of the sexes and chicken: the players disagree. Each is happier in a different equilibrium.

That answers the question at the top of this page. No. Neither evening is better for both friends. Bowling is better for him, the film is better for her, and both beat sitting apart. Your two friends are in a battle of the sexes, and the two endings are not ranked. If your first thought was "well, being together is better", that thought is right about the cells where the two of them fail to coordinate. It says nothing about how the two equilibria compare.

Then one last split, between the two disagreeing games. In battle of the sexes the equilibria are the two cells where the players choose the same action. In chicken they are the two cells where the players choose different actions. That is what the course means when it calls chicken's equilibria asymmetric.

Chicken has a third fingerprint worth knowing, because a question can be built on it. Add up both payoffs in each cell of the bridge. Either equilibrium totals −1. But (swerve, swerve), which is not an equilibrium, totals 0. So the higher joint payoff sits in a cell that is not an equilibrium. The polite outcome is the one nobody can hold.

The difference the solutions keep correcting. "Two pure equilibria" describes stag hunt, battle of the sexes and chicken. Only stag hunt's pair is Pareto-ranked: one equilibrium is better for both players at once. The 2025 C solution says it plainly: battle of the sexes has two equilibria "but neither is better for both players", and chicken's two "are not ranked in terms of Pareto efficiency". If a question says Pareto better, the answer is stag hunt every time.

Worked example

Two startups each choose whether to build on a new shared platform or stay on the old one. They only get the benefit of the new platform if both move. Payoffs (startup 1 first):
New platformOld platform
New platform6,  61,  4
Old platform4,  14,  4
Which of the five archetypes is this?
1
Check for dominance first. It is one glance and it rules out the prisoner's dilemma.
Startup 1's rows: 6 beats 4 in the left column, but 1 loses to 4 in the right one. Neither row wins everywhere.
no dominant strategy → not a prisoner's dilemma
2
Mark startup 1's best reply in each column.
Left column: 6 > 4, so new. Right column: 4 > 1, so old.
3
Mark startup 2's best reply in each row: read the second number.
Top row: 6 > 4, so new. Bottom row: 4 > 1, so old.
4
Which cells are marked twice?
(new, new) and (old, old). Two pure equilibria, so it is stag hunt, battle of the sexes or chicken.
5
Are the two ranked the same way for both players?
(6, 6) vs (4, 4) → 6 > 4 for startup 1 AND 6 > 4 for startup 2
Both prefer the same one. They agree.
✓ Stag hunt. Two pure equilibria, one Pareto better than the other. The old platform is the safe rabbit: it pays 4 whatever the other firm does. The new platform is the stag. It is better for everyone, but only if both jump.
Two neighbours are in a boundary dispute. Each can back down or escalate to a lawyer. Payoffs (neighbour 1 first):
Back downEscalate
Back down0,  0−3,  2
Escalate2,  −3−8,  −8
Which archetype, and what is the tell?
Chicken.

Best replies: in the "back down" column neighbour 1 prefers 2 to 0 → escalate. In the "escalate" column he prefers −3 to −8 → back down. Same for neighbour 2 by symmetry. The two equilibria are (escalate, back down) and (back down, escalate).

Three tells, all pointing the same way. The equilibria are off the diagonal (one yields, one pushes). Each neighbour prefers the one where he is the winner, so they are not Pareto-ranked. And (back down, back down) totals 0, beating either equilibrium's total of −1.

It is not stag hunt, because nobody agrees on which equilibrium is better. It is not battle of the sexes, because there the two equilibria would sit on the diagonal.
Two departments must pick one shared software system, and they must pick the same one or the integration fails. Payoffs (department 1 first):
System ASystem B
System A6,  41,  1
System B1,  14,  6
Which archetype? And a harder follow-up: is the equilibrium at (A, A) Pareto better than the one at (B, B)?
Battle of the sexes. And no, neither equilibrium is Pareto better.

Two pure equilibria, (A, A) and (B, B), both on the diagonal. Department 1 gets 6 at (A, A) and 4 at (B, B), so it prefers A. Department 2 gets 4 and 6, so it prefers B. Each prefers a different one.

Watch the follow-up trap. The totals are equal, 10 in both equilibria, so it is tempting to say "they are the same". Pareto ranking has nothing to do with totals. It asks whether both players do better in one than in the other, and here one player always does worse. Not ranked.

If you answered stag hunt, check what you were comparing. Probably 6 against 4 for one department alone.

No stable cell at all, and what "how many equilibria" really asks P4

🎧 Listen to this section

The fifth game is the one with nothing to land on. Matching pennies: both players show a coin, player 1 wins a dollar if the two faces match, player 2 wins a dollar if they don't.

P2: headsP2: tails
P1: heads1,  −1−1,  1
P1: tails−1,  11,  −1
No green cells. Put your finger anywhere: one player always wants to move, and once he moves the other wants to move too. The chase never closes.

So there is no pure equilibrium. But there is still an equilibrium: both players choosing heads or tails at random, half and half. Nash proved every finite game has at least one equilibrium, and sometimes that one is only available in mixed strategies.

A real disagreement between your past papers, and both sides are right. The 2025 A solution lists matching pennies among the games with "a unique Nash equilibrium", and 2025 B asks directly for "the unique Nash equilibrium in the Matching Pennies game". But the 2025 C solution says matching pennies has "no pure Nash equilibria, only mixed ones". All of that is true at once, because they are counting different things: pure count = 0, total count = 1. This is exactly what a counting question tests, so read the stem before you count.
Before you count anything, find the word. "Pure" → only cells where neither player wants to move, and zero is a valid answer. "Pure or mixed" → every game has at least one, so zero is never right.

Worked example

A taxpayer decides whether to report honestly or cheat; a tax office decides whether to audit her or skip her. Auditing costs the office effort. Payoffs (taxpayer first):
AuditSkip
Report honestly0,  −10,  0
Cheat−4,  44,  −4
How many pure Nash equilibria does this game have?
1
Taxpayer's best reply in each column (first number).
If the office audits: 0 beats −4 → report honestly. If the office skips: 4 beats 0 → cheat.
2
Office's best reply in each row (second number).
If she reports honestly: 0 beats −1 → skip. If she cheats: 4 beats −4 → audit.
3
Look for a cell marked by both.
(honest, audit): she is happy, but the office would rather skip. (honest, skip): the office is happy, but she would rather cheat. (cheat, skip): she is happy, the office would rather audit. (cheat, audit): the office is happy, she would rather report honestly.
every cell has someone who wants to move
4
Say what that means, carefully.
No cell survives, so the pure count is zero. The chase pattern is the same as matching pennies even though the numbers are not symmetric.
✓ Zero pure equilibria. If the question had said "how many Nash equilibria, pure or mixed", the answer would have been one: the mixed one, where she cheats sometimes and the office audits sometimes. Same game, different basket, different number.

The whole day in one path

Four questions, in this order, name any of the five. They also tell you when a matrix is none of them. Check dominance first, because it is one glance and it settles the prisoner's dilemma before you count anything.
Two players simultaneously choose left or right. If they choose the same direction, player 1 gets 5 and player 2 gets −3. If they choose different directions, player 1 gets −3 and player 2 gets 5.

How many pure equilibria does this game have, and what type of game is it?
Zero pure equilibria. It is a zero-sum game: matching pennies wearing different numbers.

Write it out:
LeftRight
Left5,  −3−3,  5
Right−3,  55,  −3
Player 1 wants to match. Player 2 wants to miss. Same endless chase, so no cell is stable.

This is a real past-final question (2024 A), and the official answer is "it is a zero-sum game". Strictly speaking every cell sums to 2, not 0, so it is a constant-sum game. The official solution says so itself and still marks zero-sum correct, because a constant-sum game works the same way: one player's gain is exactly the other's loss. If you ever see this on a paper, pick zero-sum and do not argue.
Player 1 chooses a row, player 2 a column (player 1's payoff first):
LeftRight
Top2,  −2−1,  1
Bottom−3,  3−2,  2
It has the win-lose look of matching pennies. Does it also have no pure equilibrium?
No. It has exactly one pure equilibrium: (Top, Right).

Player 1 has a dominant strategy. Top beats Bottom in both columns: 2 > −3 and −1 > −2. So he always plays Top.

Knowing that, player 2 compares her two payoffs in the top row: 1 beats −2, so she plays Right. Neither wants to move from that cell.

The lesson: "zero-sum and win-lose" does not mean "no pure equilibrium". Check dominance before you assume you are in a chase. The same trap appears again on Day 6, where students start solving mixed-strategy equations for a game that was already settled.
