Name that game: the five game types side by side

P3P4

Your teacher splits these five games across two lectures. The exam puts them in one question and asks you to choose. Dominance and the prisoner's dilemma are about 13% of the paper. Add counting equilibria, and it is the biggest topic on the whole exam.

⏸ Guess first

Two friends want to spend the evening together. He would rather go bowling. She would rather see a film. Both would much rather be together than alone. So there are two sensible endings: both bowling, or both at the cinema.

Is one of those two endings better for both of them?

Take 20 seconds. Decide on an answer before you read on.

🎧 What tells the five games apart ~1 min

A quick map of what you'll learn today. Each section below also has its own short audio. Everything you need is printed on the page, so this day works fine on paper. Print it and the hidden answers come out with it.

The five games, by name:

🔒Prisoner's dilemma · Each player has a dominant strategy, and the cell they land in is worse for both than another cell.
🦌Stag hunt · Two stable cells, and both players agree which one is better. The better one just needs trust.
🎬Battle of the sexes · Two stable cells where the players choose the same action. Each prefers a different one.
🐔Chicken (hawk–dove) · Two stable cells where the players choose different actions: one yields, one goes. Each prefers a different one.
🪙Matching pennies · No stable cell at all. The only equilibrium is playing at random.

The four tools you sort them with:

  • Dominant strategy: a choice that gives you your best payoff no matter what the other player does. Rule: if you have one, use it.
  • Dominated strategy: one of your own moves that loses to another of your own moves, no matter what the other player does: one row pays you less than another row in every column. Rule: cross it out. It is never played.
  • Pure Nash equilibrium, called a stable cell on this page: a cell where neither player wants to move. Counting these cells is the fastest way to sort a matrix. The next panel shows the test.
  • Pareto-ranked: one outcome is better for both players than another. Not the same as "bigger total" and not the same as "better on average".

One line to hold onto: first ask who has a dominant strategy. Then count the stable cells. Then ask if the two players agree about which one is nicer.

The map of the day: four questions name any game

Four questions, asked in this order, will name any of the five games. They also tell you when a matrix is none of them. Check dominance first, because it takes one glance and it settles the prisoner's dilemma before you count anything.

First: what "stable cell" means P4

🎧 Listen to this section

A cell is one possible ending of the game: your choice together with the other player's choice.

Stable means: nobody regrets. Like seats at a dinner table. Given where everyone else sits, nobody wants to swap.

The test, for any cell:
  1. Freeze the other player's choice. Ask: knowing what he picked, would I switch mine?
  2. Freeze your choice. Ask the same question for him.
Both answers are "no, I stay": the cell is stable. The course's name for a stable cell is a pure Nash equilibrium. The same thing, in formal clothes.

Walk it once

Dana and Omer pick a cafe for tomorrow, each on their own phone tonight. Dana picks the row, Omer picks the column:

Omer: North cafeOmer: Beach cafe
Dana: North cafe2,  3  ✓4,  1
Dana: Beach cafe1,  23,  4
How to read this table. Rows: Dana's choice. Columns: Omer's choice. In each cell, Dana's payoff comes first and Omer's second. Green = the stable cell.
  • Check (Beach, Beach) = 3, 4. Freeze Omer on Beach. Dana compares her numbers in that column: switching to North gives her 4, staying gives 3. She switches. One regret is enough: not stable.
  • Check (North, North) = 2, 3. Freeze Omer on North: Dana has 2, switching gives her 1. She stays. Freeze Dana on North: Omer has 3, switching gives him 1. He stays. Nobody moves: stable.

That is the whole skill. Counting equilibria is this same test, run cell by cell, with patience. Day 3 trains it on bigger grids.

Dominance, and why a prisoner's dilemma needs two ticks P3

🎧 Listen to this section

A dominant strategy is a choice that is best for you no matter what the other player does. You never need to guess. Compare your own rows: one row wins in every column.

A dominated strategy is the mirror: one of your rows loses to another of your rows in every column. Cross it out. It is never played.

Have a dominant strategy? Use it. Is a row dominated? Cross it out. Neither rule needs any guess about the other player.

The story from 1950. Two prisoners, held apart. Both stay quiet: one year each. You testify against your partner: you go free, he gets twenty. Both testify: five years each.

Testifying is dominant. It shortens your sentence in both cases. So both testify, and both serve five years instead of one. And note: trust does not fix this. Even if you are sure your partner stays quiet, testifying still pays you more. The dilemma runs on temptation, not on distrust.

The exam's numbers

The course's version, from Presentation 3. Two rival clothes firms sell from printed winter catalogues, so each price is locked for the season. Each firm prints $80 (cooperate) or $70 (defect). Profits in thousands.

✓ Yes, a prisoner's dilemma. Both ticks passed. Skip the second tick and you will call half the exam's matrices a dilemma.
Where two dominant strategies meet is always a Nash equilibrium. In a dilemma it is a bad one. When that same cell is also the best for both players, it is not a dilemma, just an easy game with a happy ending.

The TV show Golden Balls ends with this exact game, for real money. Both split: share the prize. One steals: he takes it all. Both steal: nothing. For an average prize of $20,000, in thousands:

What is best for each player alone pulls both of them toward a cell that is bad for both. About half of the players split anyway.

One honest detail, once. In the bottom row, steal gives 20 against split but 0 against steal, the same as splitting would give. So stealing is only weakly dominant (never worse, sometimes better), and this matrix has three stable cells, not one. No past-paper matrix has ties like this, so the two-tick test works cleanly on the exam. Notice it, then move on.
Two neighbouring shops each decide whether to advertise. Payoffs (shop 1 first, in thousands):

Run both ticks on this matrix. Is it a prisoner's dilemma?
No.

First tick passes: advertising is dominant for shop 1 (5 > 2 and 3 > 1), and the same for shop 2. Both advertise and both get 5.

Second tick: is any cell better for both than 5? Look: 3, 2, 1… no. It fails.

So (advertise, advertise) is still a Nash equilibrium, just a happy one. The game has an obvious answer, not a dilemma. Dominance alone is never enough. That gap is exactly where the exam sets its trap.
Two suppliers send parts to the same assembly line. Each decides in private: deliver on time, or let the shared deadline slip. Slipping saves you effort but delays the other supplier badly. In weeks of delay: both deliver, 2 weeks each. One slips: he waits 0, the one let down waits 12. Both slip: 6 weeks each.

Build the payoff matrix yourself, then find the equilibrium and say which cell both suppliers would prefer.
First the sign. The story is told in delay, and more delay is worse. So each payoff is minus the weeks:
DeliverSlip
Deliver−2,  −2−12,  0
Slip0,  −12−6,  −6
How to read this table. Rows: supplier 1's choice. Columns: supplier 2's choice. In each cell, supplier 1's number comes first. Each number is minus the weeks of delay, so closer to zero is better. Green = the equilibrium.
Equilibrium: (slip, slip). Slipping is dominant for each supplier: 0 beats −2, and −6 beats −12.

(deliver, deliver) gives −2 each, which beats −6 for both. Two ticks: a prisoner's dilemma, with no prison in it.

The habit here is the sign. When a story is told in costs (weeks late, money lost, years served), turn it into negative payoffs before comparing anything. Otherwise every comparison reads backwards.

Two equilibria: the only question is how they are ranked P4

🎧 Listen to this section

Three of the five games have exactly two pure equilibria. Counting alone cannot tell them apart. You need one more question, always the same one:

Two pure equilibria? Ask: is one of them better for both players?
Yes → stag hunt. No → battle of the sexes or chicken.

The pattern, left to right. Stag hunt: the players agree which equilibrium is better. The only problem is trust. Battle of the sexes and chicken: the players disagree. Each is happier in a different equilibrium.

Stag hunt breaks on fear. The prisoner's dilemma breaks on temptation. A trust problem and an incentive problem are not the same thing.

That answers the question at the top of the page. No. Bowling is better for him, the film is better for her. Both evenings beat being apart, but neither is better for both. The friends are in a battle of the sexes: two good endings, not ranked. "Being together is better" is true against the apart cells. It says nothing about the two good endings against each other.

One last split. Battle of the sexes: the equilibria are same-action cells, together on the diagonal. Chicken: they are different-action cells: one yields, one goes. That is what the course means by asymmetric equilibria.

Chicken has a third sign. Add both payoffs in each cell of the bridge: either equilibrium totals −1. (Swerve, swerve) totals 0, and it is not an equilibrium. The best cell for the pair sits where nobody can hold it.

The difference the solutions keep correcting. "Two pure equilibria" fits stag hunt, battle of the sexes and chicken. Only in stag hunt is one equilibrium better for both players. That is what Pareto better means. So when a question says Pareto better, the answer is stag hunt. Every time.

✓ Stag hunt. Two pure equilibria, one Pareto better than the other. The old platform is the safe rabbit: it pays 4 whatever the other firm does. The new platform is the stag. It is better for everyone, but only if both firms move.
Two neighbours are in a boundary dispute. Each can back down or escalate to a lawyer. Payoffs (neighbour 1 first):
Back downEscalate
Back down0,  0−3,  2
Escalate2,  −3−8,  −8
How to read this table. Rows: neighbour 1's choice. Columns: neighbour 2's choice. In each cell, neighbour 1's payoff comes first and neighbour 2's second.
Which archetype, and what is the tell?
Chicken.

Best replies: in the "back down" column neighbour 1 prefers 2 to 0 → escalate. In the "escalate" column he prefers −3 to −8 → back down. Same for neighbour 2 by symmetry. The two equilibria are (escalate, back down) and (back down, escalate).

Three tells, all pointing the same way. The equilibria are off the diagonal (one backs down, one pushes). Each neighbour prefers the one where he is the winner, so they are not Pareto-ranked. And (back down, back down) totals 0, beating either equilibrium's total of −1.

It is not stag hunt, because nobody agrees on which equilibrium is better. It is not battle of the sexes, because there the two equilibria would sit on the diagonal.
Two departments must pick one shared software system, and they must pick the same one or the integration fails. Payoffs (department 1 first):
System ASystem B
System A6,  41,  1
System B1,  14,  6
How to read this table. Rows: department 1's choice. Columns: department 2's choice. In each cell, department 1's payoff comes first and department 2's second.
Which archetype? And a harder follow-up: is the equilibrium at (A, A) Pareto better than the one at (B, B)?
Battle of the sexes. And no, neither equilibrium is Pareto better.

Two pure equilibria, (A, A) and (B, B), both on the diagonal. Department 1 gets 6 at (A, A) and 4 at (B, B), so it prefers A. Department 2 gets 4 and 6, so it prefers B. Each prefers a different one.

Watch the follow-up trap. The totals are equal, 10 in both equilibria, so it is tempting to say "they are the same". Pareto ranking has nothing to do with totals. It asks whether both players do better in one than in the other, and here one player always does worse. Not ranked.

If you answered stag hunt, check what you were comparing. Probably 6 against 4 for one department alone.

No stable cell at all, and what "how many equilibria" really asks P4

🎧 Listen to this section

The fifth game is the one with nothing to land on. Matching pennies: both players show a coin. Player 1 wins a dollar if the two faces match. Player 2 wins a dollar if they don't.

P2: headsP2: tails
P1: heads1,  −1−1,  1
P1: tails−1,  11,  −1
No green cells. Put your finger anywhere: one player always wants to move, and once he moves the other wants to move too. The chase never closes.

So there is no pure equilibrium. But there is still one: both players play at random, half and half. That is a mixed strategy: random play with fixed proportions. And Nash proved every finite game has at least one equilibrium somewhere.

Two past papers seem to disagree, and both are right. One lists matching pennies with "a unique Nash equilibrium". Another says it has no pure equilibria. They count different things: pure count = 0, total count = 1. Read the question's words before you count.
Before you count anything, find the word. "Pure" → only cells where neither player wants to move, and zero is a valid answer. "Pure or mixed" → every game has at least one, so zero is never right.

The four-question map sits at the top of this page. Run it with your finger on the two matrices below before you reveal anything.

Two players simultaneously choose red or blue. If they choose the same colour, player 1 gets 4 and player 2 gets −1. If they choose different colours, player 1 gets −1 and player 2 gets 4.

How many pure equilibria does this game have, and what type of game is it?
Zero pure equilibria. It is matching pennies wearing different numbers.

Write it out:
RedBlue
Red4,  −1−1,  4
Blue−1,  44,  −1
How to read this table. Rows: player 1's choice. Columns: player 2's choice. In each cell, player 1's payoff comes first and player 2's second. Player 1 wants the colours to match. Player 2 wants them to differ.
Player 1 wants to match. Player 2 wants to miss. Same endless chase, so no cell is stable.

Now the naming, which is the real trap here. Every cell sums to 3, not 0. So this is a constant-sum game, not strictly a zero-sum one. But a constant-sum game works exactly like a zero-sum game: what one player gains, the other loses. So if a question offers you zero-sum and every cell of the matrix adds up to the same number, pick zero-sum and do not argue about the constant.
Player 1 chooses a row, player 2 a column (player 1's payoff first):
LeftRight
Top2,  −2−1,  1
Bottom−3,  3−2,  2
How to read this table. Rows: player 1's choice. Columns: player 2's choice. In each cell, player 1's payoff comes first and player 2's second. Every cell adds up to 0.
It has the win-lose look of matching pennies. Does it also have no pure equilibrium?
No. It has exactly one pure equilibrium: (Top, Right).

Player 1 has a dominant strategy. Top beats Bottom in both columns: 2 > −3 and −1 > −2. So he always plays Top.

Knowing that, player 2 compares her two payoffs in the top row: 1 beats −2, so she plays Right. Neither wants to move from that cell.

The lesson: "zero-sum and win-lose" does not mean "no pure equilibrium". Check dominance before you assume you are in a chase. The same trap appears again on Day 5, where students start solving mixed-strategy equations for a game that was already settled.
