Pure Nash I: underline and count

P4

The biggest topic on the paper: 27% of the marks, about 3.8 questions per paper, on every paper. Today has one job: make one routine so automatic that it costs you no thinking at all.

⏸ Guess first

In some game there is one cell that gives both players their highest payoff in the whole matrix. Everybody would love to land there.

Is that cell automatically the outcome the two players end up at?

Take 20 seconds. Commit to an answer before you read on.

🎧 What makes a cell an equilibrium ~1 min

A quick map of what you'll learn today. Each section below also has its own short audio.

  • Nash equilibrium: a pair of choices where each player's choice is a best reply to the other's. Nobody can do better by changing on their own.
  • Best reply: the best you can do while the other player's choice stays fixed. Not the best cell on the page. The best cell in that one column, or that one row.
  • The routine: underline player 1's best reply in every column, then player 2's best reply in every row. Both underlined = a pure Nash equilibrium. Pure means each player picks one action for certain.
  • The counting question: "how many pure Nash equilibria?" with options 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. One missed cell moves you one whole option along.
  • The follow-up: "what is the highest payoff player 1 can get in a pure Nash equilibrium?" Find them all, then look at player 1's number inside those cells only.

One line to hold onto: an equilibrium is not the best outcome. It is the outcome nobody walks away from alone.

What the equilibrium test actually asks

P4
🎧 Listen to this section

The professor's definition is one sentence long: a strategy profile where each player's strategy is a best reply to the opponent's strategy. A strategy profile is one choice for each player, so it is one cell of the grid. Everything today explains that sentence.

"Best reply" is the part worth slowing down on. It does not mean "the best cell on the page". It means: freeze what the other player did, and ask whether you could have done better. If player 2 went Left, you only ever compare the numbers in the Left column. Nothing else on the matrix exists for that comparison.

So an equilibrium is a cell where neither player has a private regret. Player 1 looks at her column and thinks: given she went there, I did as well as I could. Player 2 looks at his row and thinks the same. Nobody wants to move on their own.

The test is stability, never quality. Ask "would anyone move alone?", not "is this a nice place to be?"

Exam-style data

Two ice-cream vans park at the same beach. Each morning each owner picks a price: keep the Normal price, or Cut it to steal the other's customers. The numbers are the day's profit in hundreds of shekels: player 1 first, player 2 second.

Worked example

Using the ice-cream table above. (a) Is (Normal, Normal) a Nash equilibrium? (b) Is (Cut, Cut)? Answer each by checking one player at a time.
1
Test (Normal, Normal): freeze Boaz at Normal and look only down that column.
Ana's two numbers in the Normal column are 8 (if she keeps Normal) and 10 (if she Cuts).
10 > 8 → Ana wants to move. Test already failed.
2
One failure is enough, but check Boaz too so you see the shape.
Freeze Ana at Normal, look only across that row. Boaz gets 8 from Normal, 10 from Cut.
10 > 8 → Boaz also wants to move.
3
Test (Cut, Cut): freeze Boaz at Cut, look down the Cut column.
Ana gets 3 from Normal, 5 from Cut. She is already on the bigger one.
5 > 3 → Ana stays. ✓
4
Freeze Ana at Cut, look across the Cut row.
Boaz gets 3 from Normal, 5 from Cut. He stays too.
5 > 3 → Boaz stays. ✓
✓ (Normal, Normal) is not an equilibrium, even though it is the best cell for both. (Cut, Cut) is the equilibrium, even though both would rather be somewhere else. Good and stable are two different questions.
The trap this sets up. When a question asks "how many pure Nash equilibria?", your eye is drawn to the friendly cells: the big numbers, the cells where both do well. Those are exactly the cells most likely to fail. The routine in the next section stops your eye from choosing for you.
A different pair. Two neighbouring farms decide whether to Share a water pump or each buy their own (Alone). Profits in thousands, player 1 first:
B SharesB Alone
A Shares7,  71,  4
A Alone4,  13,  3
Here (Share, Share) is the best cell for both. Does that mean it fails the test, like the ice-cream case did?
No, here it passes. And that is the point: "best for both" tells you nothing either way. You still have to check.

Freeze B at Share: A gets 7 from Share, 4 from Alone → A stays. Freeze A at Share: B gets 7 from Share, 4 from Alone → B stays. So (Share, Share) is an equilibrium.

Now check the other diagonal cell. Freeze B at Alone: A gets 1 from Share, 3 from Alone → A stays. Same for B. So (Alone, Alone) is also an equilibrium. This game has two.

Two games, two friendly-looking cells, opposite verdicts. Never guess from the shape of the numbers. Mark, then read.

The routine: columns for player 1, rows for player 2

P4
🎧 Listen to this section

Checking cells one at a time works, but it is slow and you will miss one. The professor's method does the whole matrix in two sweeps, and it is the only thing you need to be fast at today.

Sweep one: go down the columns. Take one column. Ask: if player 2 chose this column, which row is best for player 1? Underline player 1's number there. Repeat for every column.

Sweep two: go across the rows. Take one row. Ask: if player 1 chose this row, which column is best for player 2? Underline player 2's number there. Repeat for every row.

Now look for cells where both numbers are underlined. Each one is a pure Nash equilibrium. That's it. No more thinking needed.

Say it while your pen moves: columns for the row player, rows for the column player. Drifting between the two is the whole error.

Worked example

Using the food-truck matrix above: find every pure Nash equilibrium, and say how many there are.
1
Column "Gate": read only Ana's numbers going down.
Ana gets 3, 9, 5. The biggest is 9.
underline Ana's 9 in (Stage, Gate)
2
Column "Stage", then column "Lake": same question each time.
Stage column: Ana gets 4, 2, 8 → biggest 8. Lake column: Ana gets 6, 5, 4 → biggest 6.
underline Ana's 8 in (Lake, Stage) and her 6 in (Gate, Lake)
3
Now turn sideways. Row "Gate": read only Boaz's numbers going across.
Boaz gets 2, 5, 7. The biggest is 7.
underline Boaz's 7 in (Gate, Lake)
4
Rows "Stage" and "Lake": same question.
Stage row: Boaz gets 3, 6, 1 → biggest 6. Lake row: Boaz gets 4, 9, 2 → biggest 9.
underline Boaz's 6 in (Stage, Stage) and his 9 in (Lake, Stage)
5
Read off the cells carrying both marks.
(Gate, Lake) has Ana's 6 and Boaz's 7. (Lake, Stage) has Ana's 8 and Boaz's 9. No other cell has two.
✓ Two pure Nash equilibria: (Gate, Lake) with payoffs (6, 7), and (Lake, Stage) with payoffs (8, 9).
Notice the near-miss. Ana's biggest number anywhere is the 9 at (Stage, Gate), and it is underlined. It looks like a find. But Boaz's number there is 3, and his row says he would rather be at the Stage. One mark is not two. This near-miss is exactly the cell that turns a count of two into a wrong count of three.

Bigger grids, counting, and the follow-up question

P4
🎧 Listen to this section

A four-by-four is not harder than a three-by-three. It is the same two sweeps, just more of them: four columns and four rows instead of three and three. What changes is the risk. Sixteen cells is enough marking for attention to slip, and the slip always happens in the last column.

And here is why a slip is expensive. The counting question offers you 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. Whatever number you produce, it is on the list. There is no ridiculous option to warn you that you went wrong. Miss one equilibrium and you confidently pick the wrong answer.

Finish all the marking, then count. Never count as you go. That is how a cell in the last column gets skipped.

Worked example

Using the four-by-four above: (a) how many pure Nash equilibria does the game have? (b) What is the highest payoff player 1 can obtain in a pure Nash equilibrium?
1
Sweep the four columns, reading only player 1's numbers.
W: 1, 2, 3, 0 → C. X: 7, 3, 9, 2 → C. Y: 2, 6, 4, 5 → B. Z: 5, 1, 7, 10 → D.
solid marks at (C,W) (C,X) (B,Y) (D,Z)
2
Sweep the four rows, reading only player 2's numbers.
A: 5, 2, 6, 3 → Y. B: 4, 1, 7, 2 → Y. C: 8, 5, 3, 6 → W. D: 2, 9, 4, 7 → X.
dotted marks at (A,Y) (B,Y) (C,W) (D,X)
3
Compare the two lists. A cell in both lists is an equilibrium.
Solid: (C,W) (C,X) (B,Y) (D,Z). Dotted: (A,Y) (B,Y) (C,W) (D,X). Appearing in both: (B,Y) and (C,W).
answer to (a): 2
4
Now the follow-up. Read player 1's number in those two cells only.
(B,Y) gives player 1 a 6. (C,W) gives player 1 a 3. Ignore every other cell on the grid.
max(6, 3) = 6
✓ (a) 2 pure Nash equilibria. (b) The highest player 1 can obtain in one is 6, not the 10 at (D,Z), and not the 9 at (C,X). Both of those are underlined for player 1, and neither is an equilibrium.
Why the follow-up is worth its own habit. Every wrong number on the option list is a real number from the grid: player 1's overall maximum, player 1's payoff in the other equilibrium, and player 2's payoff in the right equilibrium. (The fifth option is usually "the game does not admit a pure Nash equilibrium".) Grabbing any of the numbers feels like reading carefully. The only defence is the order: find all equilibria first, then look inside them.
Same four-by-four, one word changed: what is the highest payoff player 2 can obtain in a pure Nash equilibrium?
8. The equilibria don't move. They are still (B,Y) and (C,W). Only the number you read changes.

Player 2 gets 7 at (B,Y) and 8 at (C,W). The biggest is 8.

Look at what just happened: player 1's best equilibrium and player 2's best equilibrium are different cells. Player 1 prefers (B,Y); player 2 prefers (C,W). So you cannot solve for one player and reuse the answer for the other, and the exam does ask both versions. Check who is named in the question before you read a single number.

The same grid comes back wearing a different coat

This last section is not about method. It is about the one habit that protects the method: never answer from memory.

One particular three-by-three has now appeared on three sittings in a row: May 2025, June 2025 and August 2025. Same nine cells, same nine pairs of numbers. Two things move between sittings: where the equilibrium sits, and which player is being asked about. Neither is something you can remember. Both are something you can mark.

Worked example

Take arrangement two. Find the unique pure Nash equilibrium, and give the payoff of player 2 there.
1
Columns, player 1's numbers only. Read straight down, never across.
Left: 9, 8, 10. Center: 4, 5, 2. Right: 7, 8, 7.
Left → Down (10) · Center → Middle (5) · Right → Middle (8)
2
Rows, player 2's numbers only.
Up: 8, 7, 9 → Right. Middle: 3, 4, 1 → Center. Down: 6, 7, 6 → Center.
Up → Right (9) · Middle → Center (4) · Down → Center (7)
3
Cells in both lists.
Player 1's marks: (Down, Left), (Middle, Center), (Middle, Right). Player 2's marks: (Up, Right), (Middle, Center), (Down, Center). Only (Middle, Center) is in both.
4
Read the number the question asked for.
The cell holds (5, 4). Player 1's payoff is 5; player 2's is 4. The question named player 2.
✓ Unique pure equilibrium (Middle, Center). Player 2's payoff is 4. Had the question named player 1, the answer would have been 5, and both numbers are on the option list.
The sharpest argument for re-solving. The sentence "What is the highest payoff that Rowana (player 1, the row player) can obtain in a pure Nash equilibrium?" appears word for word in the June 2024 paper and again in the August 2025 paper. The correct answers are different, 1 in one paper and 7 in the other, because the matrix underneath is a different matrix. If you ever recognise a question and answer it from memory, this is the question that will punish you.
And don't trust a published solution's labels. The published solution for the August 2025 sitting gets the final number right (4) but says the equilibrium is (Up, Center), where "Player 1 gets 7 and Player 2 gets 4". Both of those are wrong. The equilibrium is (Middle, Center), and it holds (5, 4).

Here is how that slip happened, and it is worth understanding. In the printed grid the two payoffs are stacked, not side by side. And player 2's number sits on top, player 1's underneath. At (Up, Center) the stack reads 7 over 4. Read it top-to-bottom as "player 1, then player 2" and you get exactly the sentence in that solution. The author picked the wrong cell and read the stack in the wrong order, and the two mistakes cancelled into the right final answer.

So: when a grid stacks its payoffs instead of writing "3, 5", work out which number belongs to which player before you do anything else. Check it against a row where the two numbers differ obviously. This is the same ordering trap that sits under every stacked matrix on the paper.
