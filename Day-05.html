Pure Nash II: elimination

P4

Finding pure equilibria is 27% of the marks, the biggest single block on the paper. Yesterday you underlined every cell. Today you learn how to throw most of them away first.

⏸ Guess first

Two firms pick how much to produce. Producing a small quantity is your best possible move against a rival who floods the market. It earns you more than any other choice you have.

So a small quantity is a move you must obviously keep. Can it ever be right to cross it out?

Take 20 seconds. Commit to an answer before you read on.

🎧 Why elimination is safe ~1 min

A quick map of what you'll learn today. Each section below also has its own short audio.

  • Strictly dominated: another move of yours gives a strictly higher payoff against every single thing the opponent could do. Never on average. Always.
  • Weakly dominated: never better, and sometimes worse. Ties are allowed.
  • IESDS (iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies): delete a strictly dominated strategy, then look again at the smaller grid, and repeat. The order decides when each cut is allowed, so look again after every cut. But whatever order you use, you end with the same survivors. The order only changes the answer if you cut weakly dominated strategies.
  • Dominance-solvable: the cutting ends with one single cell. That cell is the unique Nash equilibrium, the one outcome nobody wants to move away from alone. No other pure one, and no mixed one either. (A mixed strategy means picking between your moves by chance.)

One line to hold onto: strict elimination throws away nothing. Weak elimination can throw away an answer.

A dominated strategy is not a bad strategy

P4
🎧 Listen to this section

Two flatmates, one shop

Dana and Rami both want a bulk deal at the shop, but it only works if both of them turn up. One person alone cannot carry it. Going costs nothing except the walk. If both go, each gains 1. Any other combination, both get 0.

Rami goesRami stays home
Dana goes1,  1  ✓0,  0
Dana stays home0,  00,  0  ✓
Two pure Nash equilibria, both marked green. Both-go is obvious. Both-stay is also stable. If Rami stays home, walking to the shop alone gains Dana nothing, so she has no reason to move either.

The word dominated sounds like an insult. It isn't. A dominated strategy can pay you well; in one column it can even pay more than the winning move pays in another. That is not the test.

The test compares two of your own strategies, column by column. Walk across every single thing the opponent might do. If one strategy wins every comparison, the other is strictly dominated. Delete it. You never need to know what the opponent will choose.

Weakly dominated = another move is never worse, and somewhere strictly better. Ties allowed.
Strictly dominant = one move of mine beats all my other moves, against every opponent strategy.

That "ties allowed" is not a small detail. It is the whole difference between strict and weak, and it is what the exam tests.

Compare Dana's two rows. Going gives her 1 when Rami goes and 0 when he stays. Staying gives 0 and 0. So going is never worse and sometimes better: weak domination. The tie in the right-hand column stops it being strict.

Delete Dana's "stays home" as weakly dominated, and Rami's too. One cell is left: both go. Clean, tidy, and wrong. You erased a real equilibrium: two flatmates each sitting at home, waiting for the other to move first, is a thing that really happens.

The exam sentence, in one line. Eliminating strictly dominated strategies never removes an equilibrium. Eliminating weakly dominated strategies can. So when you are counting equilibria, only ever cross out on strict grounds.

Worked example

In the flatmates game above, is Dana's "stays home" strictly dominated, weakly dominated, or neither? And what happens to the number of pure Nash equilibria if you delete it anyway?
1
Fix the first column: Rami goes.
Dana's payoff from going is 1; from staying it is 0.
1 > 0  →  going wins this column
2
Fix the second column: Rami stays home.
Dana's payoff from going is 0; from staying it is also 0.
0 = 0  →  a tie, not a win
3
Apply the two definitions.
Strict needs a win in every column. It failed in column two. Weak needs never-worse plus at least one strict win. Both are true here.
weakly dominated, not strictly
4
Count the equilibria before and after.
Before deleting: (go, go) and (stay, stay), so two. After deleting both players' "stay" rows and columns, only (go, go) survives, so one.
2  →  1: one equilibrium destroyed
✓ Weakly dominated, not strictly. Deleting it drops the count from 2 to 1, which is exactly why the course only lets you eliminate on strict grounds.
Read this rule first. In the table below, as the exam always says, the first number is player 1's payoff and the second is player 2's.
LeftRight
Up5,  −16,  −2
Down4,  37,  −3
Which player has a strictly dominant strategy, and what is the unique Nash equilibrium?
Player 2's dominant strategy is Left, and the unique equilibrium is (Up, Left).

Player 2 chooses the column, so only the second number in each cell is hers. Left against Right: in the Up row −1 > −2; in the Down row 3 > −3. Left wins both rows, so Left is strictly dominant. Delete Right.

Now player 1 compares his own numbers inside the surviving Left column: Up gives 5, Down gives 4. Up wins. Unique equilibrium (Up, Left), payoffs (5, −1). Player 1 has no dominant strategy of his own. Down beats Up against Right (7 > 6) but loses against Left (4 < 5).
Now a second step. A classmate answers the question above with: "Player 2's dominant strategy is Right, so the equilibrium is (Up, Right)." Every subtraction he did was correct. What went wrong?
What the classmate did: he read the pairs the wrong way round, treating the first number as player 2's. Do that and player 2 appears to compare 5 against 6, then 4 against 7, so Right looks dominant, and out comes (Up, Right). His arithmetic was perfect. He simply compared the wrong column of numbers.

Why this is the most dangerous error on the paper: it does not feel like an error. A dropped minus sign or a missed cell usually leaves you stuck, or gives you something that is not among the options. Swapping the two players gives you a clean, confident, unique answer that is sitting right there in the list. So before you compare anything, find the sentence that says which number belongs to whom. Every exam question says it. Then circle the choosing player's numbers.

The chain: delete, look again, delete again

P4
🎧 Listen to this section

The data: Cournot competition (Presentation 4)

Cournot competition: two firms choose how much to produce, and the market price falls as total production rises.

Two firms sell the same good. Marginal cost is $20 each. The price falls as total output rises: P = 140 − Q₁ − Q₂ so each firm's profit is πᵢ = (120 − Qⱼ − Qᵢ)·Qᵢ. To keep it a matrix, each firm can only choose 30, 35, 40, 50 or 60 units.

Row = firm 1's quantity, column = firm 2's quantity. Each cell shows (firm 1's profit, firm 2's profit). The grid below comes straight from that profit formula. For example row 40, column 50: P = 140 − 40 − 50 = 50, so firm 1 earns (50 − 20) × 40 = 1200. The game is symmetric: whatever is dominated for one firm is dominated for the other. So every cut removes a row and the matching column at once.

Still alive:
Play it once through. Each round, find the dominated quantity yourself first. The page shows the check only after you pick.

Worked example

Run iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies on the Cournot grid above. Which quantities survive, and in what order do the others go?
✓ Four rounds, in this order: 60, then 30, then 50, then 35. That leaves a single cell: (40, 40), worth 1600 to each firm. Each round cuts a row and a column, so that is eight strike-throughs. Losing count of them is the real risk here, not the arithmetic.
How to stay fast and not lose your place. Don't try to hold the chain in your head. After each cut write one short line on your draft paper (still alive: 30 35 40 50) and only compare inside that list. On a 5×5, one written line per round costs you about ten seconds and saves you the re-count that costs two minutes.
A student looks at the full 5×5 Cournot grid at the very start and says: "30 is obviously terrible. It never gives the highest profit in any column. Let me delete 30 first and save time."

Is she allowed to? And what is the flaw in her reasoning?
No, she is not allowed to. Her test is the wrong test.

Look at the 60 column in the original grid. Quantity 30 earns 900 there; 35 earns 875, 40 earns 800, 50 earns 500, 60 earns 0. So in that column, 30 is not just good enough. It is the best row on the board. Nothing dominates it. It cannot be deleted.

The flaw: "never gives the highest profit in any column" is a different claim from "some single other strategy beats it in every column". Domination compares two of your own strategies, column by column. It does not compare one strategy against the best of everything. Her sentence isn't even true here. And even when it is true, it isn't enough.

And notice the order she wanted has the right instinct hiding inside it: 30 does go second. She just cannot get there until the 60 column is gone.

One cell left means one equilibrium: pure and mixed

P4P1
🎧 Listen to this section

When the chain ends with exactly one surviving cell, the course calls the game dominance-solvable. The Cournot grid above is one. And that surviving cell is not just an equilibrium. It is the only equilibrium the game has.

Not "the only pure one". The only one. Full stop. There is no mixed equilibrium sitting somewhere else.

Dominance-solvable → the surviving cell is the unique Nash equilibrium. No other pure equilibria, and no mixed equilibria either.

Why not? A mixed strategy is a recipe built from the pure strategies you are willing to use. Each deleted strategy lost against everything, so none can carry weight in a mixture. There is nothing left to mix with.

The most likely trap on your paper. The question is worded "How many Nash equilibria (pure or mixed) exist?" and the options run 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. That phrase "pure or mixed" whispers surely there's a mixed one too. People who did all the elimination correctly then answer 2 instead of 1. Nash's theorem says every game has at least one. It never says more than one.

Worked example

A different game. Row player chooses Up, Mid or Down; column player chooses Left, Center or Right. First number is the row player's payoff.
LeftCenterRight
Up4, 71, 52, 1
Mid6, 25, 63, 4
Down2, 80, 97, 5
How many Nash equilibria (pure or mixed) does this game have?
1
Start with the row player. Compare Up and Mid.
Up gives 4, 1, 2. Mid gives 6, 5, 3.
6 > 4, 5 > 1, 3 > 2 → Up is strictly dominated by Mid. Cut Up.
2
Now the column player, in the 2×3 that is left (rows Mid and Down). Compare Left and Center.
Left gives her 2 and 8. Center gives her 6 and 9.
6 > 2, 9 > 8 → Left is strictly dominated by Center. Cut Left.
Note this was not true before: in the Up row, Left gave 7 and Center only 5. The row cut unlocked it.
3
Still the column player. Compare Right and Center.
Right gives 4 and 5. Center gives 6 and 9.
6 > 4, 9 > 5 → Right is strictly dominated by Center. Cut Right.
4
One column left. Back to the row player.
In the Center column, Mid gives 5 and Down gives 0.
5 > 0 → Down is strictly dominated. Cut Down. One cell remains: (Mid, Center).
5
Answer the question that was actually asked.
The game is dominance-solvable, so (Mid, Center) is the unique Nash equilibrium, pure and mixed.
count = 1
✓ Exactly one: the pure equilibrium (Mid, Center), payoffs (5, 6). Nothing mixed hides behind it, because every other strategy was thrown out for losing everywhere.

The same chain in the guessing game you already played

🎧 Listen to this section

The 2/3-average contest from Presentation 1: everyone picks a whole number from 1 to 100, and whoever is closest to two-thirds of the average wins. The same elimination runs there, just a long way.

RoundWhat gets deletedWhat is left
168–100, dominated by 671–67
246–67, dominated by 451–45
331–45, dominated by 301–30
421–30, dominated by 201–20
515–20, dominated by 141–14
610–14, dominated by 91–9

112, dominated by 11  ✓
Eleven rounds, same rule every time. Whatever the others do, two-thirds of the average can never reach the top of the range. So the top of the range can always be beaten by aiming lower. The unique Nash equilibrium is everyone choosing 1, which almost nobody actually plays.
In real experiments with the 2/3-average game, only about one player in ten actually chooses 1. Most answers sit well above it.

Does that mean the elimination argument above is wrong, and that 1 is not really the Nash equilibrium?
No. The equilibrium is still 1. The experiment measures something different.

Nash equilibrium asks: if everyone were already doing this, would anyone want to change? With everyone at 1, the average is 1, two-thirds of it is below 1, and 1 is the closest legal number. Nobody gains by moving. That is the definition, and it holds regardless of what people actually do.

What the experiment shows is how many rounds of the reasoning real people actually do. Most stop after one or two: "others will pick around fifty, so I'll pick around thirty-three"; "no, others will think that, so I'll pick around twenty-two". Very few keep going all eleven rounds. Play the game repeatedly and answers drift downward, slowly, toward 1.

Exam note: the course tests both facts, and they are different questions. "What is the unique Nash equilibrium?" → everyone chooses 1. "What do people actually choose?" → most stop after one or two rounds of the reasoning, so their answers sit well above 1, around 33 and around 22. Read which one is being asked.

The smooth version: where the two best-reply lines cross

P4
🎧 Listen to this section

Drop the made-up menu of five quantities and let each firm choose any number at all. Then instead of a grid you get a curve. Your best reply is simply your best move against what the other side does. Firm i's profit is (120 − Qⱼ − Qᵢ)·Qᵢ. This is a parabola in Qᵢ that hits zero at 0 and at 120 − Qⱼ. Its peak is exactly halfway between:

BR(Qⱼ) = (120 − Qⱼ) / 2
Each line answers "given what the other firm does, what is my best output?" Only where they cross is each firm best-replying to the other at the same time. That crossing is (40, 40), the exact cell the elimination chain left standing on the grid above.

Formula sheet

The whole course on one page. A study aid only: the exam is closed book, so learn these by heart.

1. Sequential games (P2)

  • Backward induction: solve the last node first. Replace it with its payoff pair. Walk back to the start
  • Strategy = a plan for every node you could reach, not just one move
  • A tree that looks like a centipede is not always one. Only the payoffs decide

2. Prisoner's dilemma (P3)

  • R both cooperate · T you cheat alone · S you cooperate alone · P both defect. In a dilemma: T > R > P > S
  • Two-tick test: (1) both players have a dominant strategy, and (2) where they land is worse for both than another cell
  • Known last round: defect in every round (backward induction)
  • No end date: a payment of x every year forever is worth x / r today
  • Tipping point: r* = (R − P) / (T − R). Below r*: cooperate. Above: defect
  • Lab (Mengel 2018): waste (R − P) / R big → more cooperation · risk (P − S) / P big → less cooperation · temptation (T − R) / T small effect
  • About 45% cooperate in round 1, falling about 2% per round

3. Nash equilibrium (P4)

  • Underline: columns down for player 1 (first number), rows across for player 2 (second number). Two marks = pure Nash equilibrium
  • Test is stability, not quality: “would anyone move alone?”
  • “Pure” → zero is possible. “Pure or mixed” → every finite game has at least one
  • Strictly dominated = always worse. Weakly = never better, sometimes worse. Dominance-solvable → one unique equilibrium
  • Cournot: P = a − Q₁ − Q₂ · profit (P − c) × Q

4. Mixed strategies and zero-sum (P5)

  • First check: a dominant strategy? A saddle point? Neither → mix
  • Indifference: make the opponent's two expected payoffs equal. Solve for your p
  • Expected payoff of a row: q × (left) + (1 − q) × (right)
  • Chance of one cell: p × q, or p × (1 − q) when crossed
  • Value (player 1's payoff) = expected payoff of either of her rows
  • Zero-sum: one number per cell. Player 2 gets minus that

5. The 2/3-average game (P1, P4)

  • Average of a uniform range: (min + max) / 2
  • Mixed crowd: share₁ × avg₁ + share₂ × avg₂, then × 2/3 once, at the end
  • Nash equilibrium = 1

6. Credible strategies (P6)

  • Threat test: “would I really carry it out when the moment came?”
  • A move works only if made before the other side chooses, and visible to them
  • 8 devices: contracts · reputation · cutting off communication · burning bridges · leaving the outcome beyond your control · moving in small steps · teamwork · mandated negotiating agents

7. Lab numbers

  • Ultimatum: median offer about 40% (most common 50%; offers under 20% often rejected)
  • Dictator: median about 25% · Golden Balls: about 50% split
