The two arithmetic questions you must not lose

P5P1P4

Today you do not learn the method again: you drill it and hunt the traps. Every paper puts two questions on one grid, worth 14 points together, and both rest on a single equation.

⏸ Guess first

A mixed strategy means you do not always play the same action. You pick at random, with fixed chances. A mixed equilibrium is when both sides do that and neither one wants to change.

Here player 1 plays Top two times out of three. Player 2 plays Left two times out of three.

Quick, without a calculator: how often do the two of them land on the cell (Top, Right)? Is it about two out of three? One out of three? Or something smaller than both?

Take 20 seconds. Commit to an answer before you read on.

🎧 The two questions that travel together ~1 min

Thirty minutes, two halves, no new theory. Each section below has its own short audio.

  • The pair: every paper carries two questions on the same zero-sum grid, usually late on. Zero-sum means what one player wins, the other loses. First comes the probability of one outcome, then player 1's expected payoff, which is the average payoff per play. One bad equation loses both.
  • A cell is a product: the probability of an outcome is a row chance times a column chance. Matching cell: p × q. Mismatched cell: p × (1−q).
  • The shape trap: most past grids have zeros on the main diagonal, and there the two mixtures come out equal. Two past grids have a different shape. On those, equal mixtures are simply wrong.
  • Average first: in the 2/3-average contest, combine the whole crowd into one average, then take two-thirds. Once, at the end.
  • Equilibrium is not experiment: the equilibrium of the contest is 1. In the lab, most people stop after one or two steps of reasoning. That is what people do, not what the theory says.

One line to hold onto: both halves fail the same way. You stop one step too early, and that half-finished number is on the option list.

One grid, two questions, and a cell is a product

P5
🎧 Listen to this section

Every past paper does the same thing, usually near the end: one small zero-sum grid, then two questions back to back. First: what is the probability that the outcome is (this row, that column)? Second: what is player 1's expected payoff? An expected payoff is an average: what you earn per play over many plays.

You solve the grid once. Two indifference equations give two numbers: how often player 1 plays the top row, and how often player 2 plays the left column. Indifference means a player does not care which action she takes, because both pay her the same. Together the two numbers form one Nash equilibrium: a pair of strategies where neither side can do better by changing alone. If the calculation is right, both answers fall out. If it is wrong, both fall over. That is why 14 points sit on one piece of arithmetic, the biggest single risk on the paper.

Now the part that costs marks. You worked out that player 1 plays Top 80% of the time. The option list has 80% on it, and taking it feels safe. It is wrong. The question asked about a cell, not a row: a row and a column at the same time. The two players choose separately, so you multiply.

The chance of a row is not the chance of an outcome. An outcome needs both: P(row, column) = P(row) × P(column). Matching cell → p × q. Mismatched cell → p × (1−q).

The grid, with the mixtures written on the margins

There is one number per cell, and that number is player 1's payoff. Player 1 picks the row and wants it high. Player 2 picks the column and wants it low. Write the mixtures in the margins before you start, the way the professor does in his own solutions:

Leftplayed with prob. qRightplayed with prob. 1−q
Topprob. p50
Downprob. 1−p020

p and 1−p are player 1's mixture. q and 1−q are player 2's mixture. There are only ever these two unknowns in a 2×2.

One vertical cut at q, one horizontal cut at p. The area of a rectangle is the probability of that outcome. The big purple rectangle and the thin amber one beside it both start with the same p, yet their sizes are very different. Only the column name in the brackets tells you which one the question wants.

Trap 1: half the past questions ask for a mismatched cell

Six of these probability questions sit across the past papers, and they split down the middle.

Three ask for a matching cell. Both players pick the same side, so you multiply p × q. Those three are (right, right), (Top, left) and both players play left.

Three ask for a mismatched cell, also called a crossed cell. The players pick different sides, so you multiply one chance by the leftover of the other: (Left, right) is p times the leftover of q, not p × q. Those three are (Left, right), (Top, Right) and (Down, Left).

Nothing in the question warns you which kind you have. At exam speed the two look the same.

Before you multiply, say the profile out loud: row name, column name. Then ask "same side or crossed?" That one second is worth 7 points.

Trap 2: the rounding instruction changes from paper to paper

The instruction in brackets is never the same twice. It is often different for the two questions on the same grid:

The wording above is what the exam sheets themselves print. The 2025 A detailed-solution document writes the same two questions as "rounded up to the closest percentage point" and "rounded to one digit after the point", so even the solution does not repeat the paper word for word. Read the rounding instruction on the paper in front of you. Never assume it is the one you practised on.
"Round up" does not mean round up. Read it word for word and 11.11% would become 12%, and 1.333 would become 1.34. No past answer key does that. They all round the ordinary way, up or down to whichever number is nearer. So do not let the wording talk you out of a correct number. The five options are always far apart. Once your arithmetic is right, the nearest option is the one to pick.

Worked example

Use the grid above (one number per cell = player 1's payoff; Top/Down are player 1's actions, Left/Right are player 2's):

(a) What is the probability that the outcome is (Top, Right) in the mixed equilibrium? (Rounded to the nearest percentage point.)
(b) What is player 1's expected payoff in the Nash equilibrium?
1
Check there is no pure equilibrium first.
A pure equilibrium would be a single cell that neither player wants to leave. No cell here is both the smallest in its row and the largest in its column. So nobody can just sit still, and mixing is really needed.
2
Find q, the mixture that makes player 1 indifferent.
Player 1's payoff from Top is 5q + 0(1−q). From Down it is 0q + 20(1−q). Set them equal.
5q = 20(1 − q)  →  25q = 20  →  q = 0.8
3
Find p, the mixture that makes player 2 indifferent.
Player 2 wants that payoff low. She is indifferent when player 1's payoff is the same in both columns. Left column: 5p + 0(1−p). Right column: 0p + 20(1−p).
5p = 20(1 − p)  →  p = 0.8
4
(a) Read the cell name, then multiply.
The cell is (Top, Right), so it is crossed. Top has probability p. Right has probability 1−q.
0.8 × (1 − 0.8) = 0.8 × 0.2 = 0.16 → 16%
5
(b) Put q back into either row to get the value of the game.
The value of the game is the average payoff the grid delivers when both sides mix correctly.
Top: 5 × 0.8 = 4   Down: 20 × 0.2 = 4
They agree. That agreement is the check that your equation was right.
✓ (a) 16%  ·  (b) player 1's expected payoff, which is the value of the game, is 4.
Note what would have gone wrong. 64% is the matching cell. 80% is the row on its own. 20% is the column on its own. All three are half-answers, and real option lists do carry exactly these.
A different zero-sum game (one number per cell = player 1's payoff):
LeftRight
Top120
Down04
(a) What is the probability of the outcome (Top, Right), rounded to the closest percentage point? (b) What is player 1's expected payoff?
(a) 19%  ·  (b) 3.

Player 1 indifferent: 12q = 4(1−q) → 16q = 4 → q = 0.25. Player 2 indifferent: 12p = 4(1−p) → p = 0.25.

(Top, Right) is crossed, so p × (1−q) = 0.25 × 0.75 = 0.1875 → 19%. Value: 12 × 0.25 = 3, and from the other row 4 × 0.75 = 3. ✓

Watch the direction. The big number is top-left, and yet Top is played only a quarter of the time. That feels backwards until you remember why you mix. The mixture is chosen to control your opponent, not to chase your own big payoff.

Where the memorised shortcut breaks

P5
🎧 Listen to this section

A real pattern in the past papers, and a trap dressed as a gift. Five of the seven past zero-sum grids have the same shape: two numbers on the main diagonal, zeros in the other two corners.

LeftRight
Topa0
Down0b

Solve that shape once in letters and you get a recipe. Both players play their first action with probability b / (a+b), and the value of the game is ab / (a+b). It works on five of the seven past grids. After that many wins in a row, you stop reading the grid at all.

The most tempting part of the recipe is that p and q come out equal. That feels like a deep truth about zero-sum games. It is not. It is an accident of that one shape, and two of the seven grids break it.

Never trust a shape. Write both indifference equations, every time. Two lines of algebra cost you thirty seconds. A wrong shape costs you 14 points.

Break 1: the zeros move to the other diagonal

One past paper moves the zeros to the other diagonal, the shape of the grid below. Solve it and the two mixtures come out different. They swap over: the chance player 1 gives to the top row is the chance player 2 gives to the right column.

Leftprob. qRightprob. 1−q
Topprob. p036
Downprob. 1−p120
A grid with the zeros on the anti-diagonal, built for this page. It looks friendly: two numbers, two zeros, nothing unusual. Everything invites the recipe, and here the recipe is wrong.

Worked example

Using the grid above: what is the probability that the outcome is (Down, Left) in the mixed equilibrium, and what is player 1's expected payoff?
1
Make player 1 indifferent to get q.
Top pays 0q + 36(1−q). Down pays 12q + 0(1−q).
36(1 − q) = 12q  →  36 = 48q  →  q = 0.75
2
Make player 2 indifferent to get p.
Left column pays 0p + 12(1−p). Right column pays 36p + 0(1−p).
12(1 − p) = 36p  →  12 = 48p  →  p = 0.25
3
Stop and look at what you have.
p = 0.25  but  q = 0.75
They are different, and they add to one. That is the mark of the anti-diagonal shape: player 1 plays Top a quarter of the time, player 2 plays Left three quarters of the time. If you had assumed p = q you would already be lost.
4
Multiply for the cell, then compute the value.
P(Down, Left) = (1 − p) × q = 0.75 × 0.75 = 0.5625 → 56%
value: Top row 36 × 0.25 = 9  ·  Down row 12 × 0.75 = 9
✓ P(Down, Left) = 56%, and player 1's expected payoff is 9. Two lines of algebra gave you both answers.
Now watch the recipe fail on this exact grid. Feed its two visible numbers into the main-diagonal formula and you get equal mixtures: 12 ÷ 48 = 25% for both players. So P(Down, Left) becomes 0.75 × 0.25 = 19% instead of 56%. On the real paper with this shape, the number the recipe gives is sitting there as one of the five options. The wrong method has its own reward waiting.

Worse: the value question that follows survives the same mistake. The value formula treats the two numbers the same way round, so the recipe still returns 9, which is correct. You check your second answer, it agrees, and you walk away confident about a first answer you have already lost.

Break 2: no zeros anywhere

The other break is simpler and more honest: a grid with four non-zero numbers. Nothing cancels, no symmetry to lean on, no recipe to reach for. One of the seven past grids has this shape. It is only frightening if you have stopped writing the equations.

A zero-sum game (one number per cell = player 1's payoff):
LeftRight
Top41
Down23
Find p, q, and player 1's expected payoff.
p = 0.25, q = 0.5, value = 2.5.

First, no pure equilibrium: Top's smallest number is 1, but 1 is not the largest in the Right column (3 is). Down's smallest is 2, and 2 is not the largest in the Left column (4 is). So mix.

q: Top pays 4q + 1(1−q) = 1 + 3q. Down pays 2q + 3(1−q) = 3 − q. Equal: 1 + 3q = 3 − q → 4q = 2 → q = 0.5.
p: Left column pays 4p + 2(1−p) = 2 + 2p. Right column pays 1p + 3(1−p) = 3 − 2p. Equal: 2 + 2p = 3 − 2p → 4p = 1 → p = 0.25.
Value: 2 + 2(0.25) = 2.5, and from the other side 1 + 3(0.5) = 2.5. ✓

The recipe? Feeding the main-diagonal numbers 4 and 3 into it gives mixtures of about 0.43 and a value of about 1.71. Not close to anything. Here the shortcut fails so loudly that you would notice. That is exactly why the anti-diagonal case is the dangerous one.

The 2/3-average contest: average first, then take two-thirds

P1P4
🎧 Listen to this section

Every single paper has one. Each player picks a whole number between 1 and 100, and whoever is closest to two-thirds of the average wins. The question tells you what everybody else is doing, and asks for your best reply: the choice that does best against what they do. Only the description of the crowd changes from year to year.

Three things have to be right. Each one is a mark that someone loses.

Thing 1: the middle of a range is not 50

If a group picks evenly from a range, the average of that group is simply (smallest + largest) ÷ 2. Read the two ends they actually gave you, not the ends of the scale.

The range they describeIts average
1 to 8945
21 to 9960, not 50
1 to 10050.5
All three ranges have appeared in a real question. The 21-to-99 one is the sharp case: it does not start at 1 or end at 100, so the habit of writing 50 is punished. And this is only the first move. The average of one group is never the answer by itself.

Thing 2: average the whole crowd first, then multiply

Most recent papers describe a crowd made of two groups: half spread over a range, half doing something else. The order you work in is the whole question.

First combine every group into one crowd average. Then take two-thirds of it, once, at the very end. Two-thirds of one group on its own is never the answer, and that number is always on the option list.
Two groups, one crowd. Take two-thirds of the band's middle on its own, leave the cluster out, and you land on the amber dot. It sits between the crowd average and the right answer. That in-between number is the one the examiner puts on the option list.

Worked example

You play the 2/3-average competition with 200 other players (each player chooses an integer between 1 and 100; whoever is closest to two-thirds of the average wins). Half of the other players choose uniformly from {11, …, 89}. The remaining half all choose 30.

What is your best reply?

Options: (a) 20   (b) 27   (c) 30   (d) 33   (e) 40
1
Average of the first group.
Uniform over 11 to 89.
(11 + 89) ÷ 2 = 50
2
Average of the second group.
Everyone picks the same number, so the group's average is that number: 30.
3
Combine into one crowd average.
The two halves are the same size, so a plain average of the two group averages is enough.
(50 + 30) ÷ 2 = 40
4
Only now, take two-thirds.
(2/3) × 40 = 26.67 → nearest whole number: 27
✓ 27.
Look at what the other options are. 40 is the crowd average, so you forgot the last step. 33 is two-thirds of 50. You took two-thirds of one group and left the other half of the crowd out. 30 is one of the groups. 20 is two-thirds of 30. Every wrong option is a half-finished version of the right answer. That is exactly how they are built.

Thing 3: equilibrium is 1, and humans stop early

Sometimes the question does not describe a crowd at all. It asks for the Nash equilibrium of the contest. That is a different question, and the answer never changes. Nobody should pick above 67, because two-thirds of any average cannot go above 66.67. With everything above 67 gone, nobody should pick above 45. Then 30, then 20, then 14 … each round strips the top away, and the only survivor is 1.

In laboratories, people do something else (Presentation 4). Most of them run one or two steps of this reasoning and then stop. The course calls them level-1 and level-2 players. If they think everyone else picks at random, level 1 picks about 34 and level 2 about 22. Only about 10% play the equilibrium. An option may give you a range of numbers and call it the equilibrium. The equilibrium is always just 1.

Two different questions, two different answers. "What is the Nash equilibrium?" → 1.   "What do people actually choose?" → they stop after one or two steps; only about 10% choose 1.
Ten players in total play the 2/3-average competition. The nine others play 5, 15, 25, 35, 45, 45, 55, 65 and 75. What is a best reply?

Options: (a) 1   (b) 26   (c) 27   (d) 33
26 and 27 both win. Two of the options are correct.

First, the thing that makes this one different. With only ten players, your own number moves the average. In the big-crowd questions there are hundreds of others. Whatever you pick barely moves the average, so you can take two-thirds of their average and stop. Here your bid is a tenth of the total, so the target moves as you move. You have to test each option.

The nine sum to 365. Bid 26 → total 391, average 39.1, target 26.07; your 26 is 0.07 away, and the nearest rival number (25) is 1.07 away. Bid 27 → total 392, average 39.2, target 26.13; your 27 is 0.87 away against 25's 1.13. Both win. Bid 33 → target 26.53, and now 25 is 1.53 away while you are 6.47 away, so you lose. Bid 1 → target 24.40, and 25 sits right beside it, so 1 is hopeless.

Two correct options can really happen. One past question of this kind has two best replies, and the professor agreed that both are right. So do not hunt for the single right answer when two of them win. Pick either and move on.
You play with 200 other players. Half of them choose uniformly from {1, …, 60}. The other half all choose 12. What is your best reply?
14.

First group's average: (1 + 60) ÷ 2 = 30.5. Second group: 12. Crowd average: (30.5 + 12) ÷ 2 = 21.25. Two-thirds of that: 14.17 → 14.

The trap number here is 20, which is two-thirds of 30.5, taken before you combined the crowd. And 21 is the crowd average with the last step forgotten. Same two mistakes, every year.

Formula sheet

The whole course on one page. A study aid only: the exam is closed book, so learn these by heart.

1. Sequential games (P2)

  • Backward induction: solve the last node first. Replace it with its payoff pair. Walk back to the start
  • Strategy = a plan for every node you could reach, not just one move
  • A tree that looks like a centipede is not always one. Only the payoffs decide

2. Prisoner's dilemma (P3)

  • R both cooperate · T you cheat alone · S you cooperate alone · P both defect. In a dilemma: T > R > P > S
  • Two-tick test: (1) both players have a dominant strategy, and (2) where they land is worse for both than another cell
  • Known last round: defect in every round (backward induction)
  • No end date: a payment of x every year forever is worth x / r today
  • Tipping point: r* = (R − P) / (T − R). Below r*: cooperate. Above: defect
  • Lab (Mengel 2018): waste (R − P) / R big → more cooperation · risk (P − S) / P big → less cooperation · temptation (T − R) / T small effect
  • About 45% cooperate in round 1, falling about 2% per round

3. Nash equilibrium (P4)

  • Underline: columns down for player 1 (first number), rows across for player 2 (second number). Two marks = pure Nash equilibrium
  • Test is stability, not quality: “would anyone move alone?”
  • “Pure” → zero is possible. “Pure or mixed” → every finite game has at least one
  • Strictly dominated = always worse. Weakly = never better, sometimes worse. Dominance-solvable → one unique equilibrium
  • Cournot: P = a − Q₁ − Q₂ · profit (P − c) × Q

4. Mixed strategies and zero-sum (P5)

  • First check: a dominant strategy? A saddle point? Neither → mix
  • Indifference: make the opponent's two expected payoffs equal. Solve for your p
  • Expected payoff of a row: q × (left) + (1 − q) × (right)
  • Chance of one cell: p × q, or p × (1 − q) when crossed
  • Value (player 1's payoff) = expected payoff of either of her rows
  • Zero-sum: one number per cell. Player 2 gets minus that

5. The 2/3-average game (P1, P4)

  • Average of a uniform range: (min + max) / 2
  • Mixed crowd: share₁ × avg₁ + share₂ × avg₂, then × 2/3 once, at the end
  • Nash equilibrium = 1

6. Credible strategies (P6)

  • Threat test: “would I really carry it out when the moment came?”
  • A move works only if made before the other side chooses, and visible to them
  • 8 devices: contracts · reputation · cutting off communication · burning bridges · leaving the outcome beyond your control · moving in small steps · teamwork · mandated negotiating agents

7. Lab numbers

  • Ultimatum: median offer about 40% (most common 50%; offers under 20% often rejected)
  • Dictator: median about 25% · Golden Balls: about 50% split
Exam: closed book, calculator allowed. This sheet is not on the mock days, so each mock tests your memory, like the real exam.
