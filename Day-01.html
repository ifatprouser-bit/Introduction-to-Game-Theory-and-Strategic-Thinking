Exam map + backward induction

P1P2

What the exam actually is, and then the cheapest marks on it: game trees. Sequential games, where players move one after another, are about 11% of the paper. Every past paper has at least one.

⏸ Guess first

A game tree has five endings. The very best ending for player 1 pays her 9. That is more than any other ending in the tree. To reach it, player 1 moves down, and then player 2 must move up. Player 2 gets 1 if he moves up, and 8 if he moves down instead.

Will player 1 walk away with 9?

Take 20 seconds. Commit to an answer before you read on.

🎧 Why you solve a tree backwards ~1 min

A quick map of the exam, then the plan for the next half hour. Each section below has its own short audio.

  • Game tree: a picture of who moves when. Forks are decisions, branches are actions, the endings carry a payoff for each player.
  • Backward induction: solve the last decision first, then the one before it, and keep walking left. The professor's phrase: look forward and reason backward.
  • Strategy: not a move. A complete plan that says what you would do at every point you could ever be asked to choose.
  • The surprising one: more freedom of action can leave you worse off, because everyone else can see what you will do with the extra options.
  • Named cases to know: Fredo & Charlie, President vs Congress, the 21-flags game, the ultimatum game, the centipede game, the 1984 Orange Bowl.

One line to hold onto: a number in the tree is not a number you can get. Only the reachable endings are real.

Reading a tree, and solving it from the last node P2

🎧 Listen to this section

The phrase sequential game with perfect information is two separate conditions. Check both.

One: players move in turn, not at the same moment. Two: when it is your turn you can see everything that has already happened: every past move, and all the payoffs.

Haggling in a market meets both. I say a price. You hear it and answer with your own. Drop either condition and the game changes. If we write our numbers and show them at the same moment, the first fails. If I see your last move but not the card in your hand, the second fails: the game is still sequential, but no longer perfect information. Telling these apart is worth a mark on its own.

These games are drawn as trees. Read them left to right, like time. Each fork is a moment where one named player chooses. Each branch is one action. Each ending on the right carries a pair of numbers: player 1's payoff first, player 2's second.

Why you must work backwards: Fredo & Charlie

Presentation 2 opens with this. A businessman in a country with weak courts (Fredo) asks a foreign investor (Charlie) to put $100,000 into a business that will return $500,000. Fredo promises to split the profit after a year.

What happensCharlieFredo
Charlie doesn't invest00
Charlie invests, Fredo honours the deal150,000250,000
Charlie invests, Fredo keeps everything−100,000500,000

Charlie cannot answer "should I invest?" until he answers "what will Fredo do once the money is here?" So settle Fredo's node first: keeping everything beats splitting. Charlie's real choice is now between losing $100,000 and getting nothing. He stays home. The unique backward-induction outcome is (0, 0). This is the game's equilibrium: the ending where no player can do better by changing his own choice. Both men would prefer the honest deal, so the lecture calls it an inefficient equilibrium.

Solve the last node first, because at the last node there is nothing left to predict. Then replace that node with the payoff pair it produces, and repeat. Keep walking left until you reach the start.

A full tree, solved

Player 1 moves first, at the round node. Player 2 moves at the square nodes. Player 1's payoff is always written first.

The thick green line is the solved path. Player 1 plays Down, player 2 plays Right, and the game ends at (3, 8). Player 1 gets 3. The grey dashed branches are what a player would choose at a node the game never reaches: they matter while you solve, but nobody walks down them. No line reaches the circled (9, 1). It is player 1's biggest payoff anywhere, but player 2 stands in the way, and he would rather have 8 than 1.

Worked example

In the tree above, what is the payoff of player 1 in the backward-induction equilibrium?
✓ The equilibrium outcome is (3, 8). Player 1 gets 3, player 2 gets 8.
Look at what you would have to choose from. An exam version of this question would offer 9, 6, 4, 3 and 2. Every one of those is a real payoff of player 1 printed in the tree. Four of them are unreachable. Recognising a number proves nothing.
Same tree, one change: the ending reached by Down, then Right, is changed from (3, 8) to (3, 0). Nothing else moves.

Now what does player 1 get?
She gets 9. Redo it from the last node. The deep node is unchanged: still worth (9, 1). Player 2, at his turn after Down, now compares 1 (from Left) with 0 (from Right) and prefers Left. So Down is worth (9, 1). At the start, Down gives player 1 nine against Up's two, so she plays Down.

One number changed at one ending, and the answer moved from 3 to 9. That is why you never guess a tree from its shape.

The three traps in a tree question P2

🎧 Listen to this section

Worked example

A chain of four turns. Player 1 moves at the round nodes, player 2 at the square nodes. At each node the player can stop, ending the game at the payoff under that node, or continue to the next node. Player 1's payoff is written first.

In Version 2 the last ending is (14, 12). Show that player 1 stops immediately, and say what each player gets. Then press Version 1, where only that last ending changes, to (14, 20).
✓ Version 2 ends at once at (4, 0): player 1 gets 4. Version 1 runs to the end at (14, 20): player 1 gets 14. One number changed, and 4 became 14.

Trap 1: the wrong options are numbers from the tree. The professor builds them from payoffs already printed in the picture, so no option ever looks wrong. The most tempting is the biggest number player 1 could ever get. If the player who moves before her would never send her down that branch, that number is only decoration.

Trap 2 is the big one, and it needs a picture. A centipede game is a long chain: at each step the player whose turn it is can stop and take a payoff, or continue and hand the decision on. The pot grows along the chain, but whoever stops takes the bigger share. In the classic version, backward induction says stop at once. That is the famous, sad result.

A tree that looks like a centipede is not always a centipede. The shape tells you nothing. Only the numbers at the endings decide.
The two versions from the worked example, side by side. Green marks the branch each player chooses at his own node. Follow green from the left-hand node to find the outcome. The two trees have exactly the same shape and exactly the same numbers, except at the very last ending: player 2's payoff there is 20 in Version 1 and 12 in Version 2. That one number decides whether player 2 continues or stops at the last node, and that decision travels back down the whole chain.
This really happened in the course's past papers. One paper has a tree of this shape, and its solution says: "Although the game looks superficially like a centipede game, it is not. [Player 2] is better off continuing in the last node, which induces both players to continue in all previous nodes." There the right answer was the largest number on the option list. In 2024 B question 6, a real centipede, the answer was the smallest option, 11, out of 11 / 17 / 24 / 29 / none of the other answers. Same shape, opposite direction. You cannot guess from the option sizes either.

Trap 3: check which player is asked. The smallest trap, and the most expensive. You can solve the whole tree correctly and still hand in the wrong half of the pair. Which player they ask for changes from paper to paper: one paper asked for player 1 (=I) in a three-player tree, another asked for player 2 (=II), a third called the players A and B. The 2024 B paper called player 1 "the red player", with his payoffs printed in red as the upper numbers. The other player's payoff is always sitting in the option list, waiting for you. Solve first, then read the question again before you pick.

In a four-node chain like the ones above, you have solved the last three nodes and found that the second node (player 2's first turn) is worth (2, 8). At the first node, player 1 can stop and take (9, 1).

Do you need to look at anything else to answer "what does player 1 get"?
No. She gets 9, and you are done. Stopping gives her 9; continuing gives her whatever the second node is worth to her, which is 2. Nine beats two, so she stops.

This is the whole point of backward induction: once a node has been replaced by its value, everything to the right of it is finished business. You never need to look at those endings again. That is exactly why the big unreachable numbers out there cannot hurt you, unless you let them.

A strategy is a whole plan, and more freedom can hurt P1P2

🎧 Listen to this section

Worked example: President vs Congress

1987, in the United States. Congress is the US parliament: it decides what public money is spent on. Two spending items are on the table. One is an antiballistic missile system, a defence project President Reagan wants. The other is urban renewal, money to rebuild poor city areas, which Congress wants. Both sides prefer both items to neither. Congress chooses what to send. The President then signs the whole package or rejects all of it: a veto. Payoffs run 4 (best) down to 1 (worst).

OutcomeCongressPresident
Both items pass33
Urban renewal only41
Missile system only14
Neither22

In his 1987 State of the Union speech (the President's yearly speech to Congress), Reagan asked for a line-item veto: the power to cut single lines instead of rejecting the whole bill. Strictly more freedom. Does it help him?

Using the payoff table above, solve both versions of the game by backward induction: (a) the President can only approve or veto the whole package; (b) the President has a line-item veto and can delete single items. Compare the two outcomes.
✓ With the line-item veto the outcome falls from (3, 3) to (2, 2). That is Pareto-worse: worse for both players, including the President who gained the power. The lecture's insight: greater freedom of action can hurt a player.

Why? Because in a sequential game your options are also a promise. Once the President can cut the package apart, Congress knows he will, so Congress never offers it. Losing the ability to do something can be worth more than the ability itself.

What a strategy is

Presentation 1 gives the definition you must hold onto: a strategy is a rule determining a player's move in all possible cases. Not "what I will do", but "what I would do, everywhere I might be asked". Think of it as written instructions you hand to a stranger before leaving the room: he must be able to finish the game without phoning you.

This is why counting strategies gives bigger numbers than people expect. If player 2 moves at three different nodes and has two actions at each, he has 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 strategies. That holds even though he only ever makes one move in a single play of the game. Multiply across the nodes. Never add.

Move = one action at one node. Strategy = a complete instruction sheet covering every node you could reach, including the ones you expect never to visit.

Long games: the poison positions in the 21-flags game

Two players, 21 flags. On your turn you remove 1, 2 or 3 flags. Whoever takes the last flag wins. The tree is far too large to draw. But backward reasoning still works, because you only need to know which positions you lose from.

The four is not magic. It is the most you may take, plus one. Whatever your opponent removes from a group of four, you remove the rest of that group. So you can hand her a multiple of four every single turn, until she faces four flags with no safe move.

This is the part that travels. Change how many flags a player may remove, and every poison position moves with it. The board can look identical and the answer can flip:

The red columns are the multiples of 4. If it is your turn and the count is a multiple of 4, you lose against a player who knows this. Otherwise, take enough to leave a multiple of 4, and keep doing it. Note that 21 is not a multiple of 4, so the player who moves first wins: take 1, leave 20.
You are playing the 21-flags game. It is your turn and 14 flags remain. How many do you take, and why?
Take 2, leaving 12. Twelve is a multiple of 4, so your opponent is now standing on a poison position. Whatever she removes (1, 2 or 3), you remove what completes 4 (3, 2 or 1), and you hand her 8, then 4, then 0. She never takes the last flag.

Check the other moves. Taking 1 leaves 13, and taking 3 leaves 11. Neither is a multiple of 4, so she can put you back on one.
Same game, one rule changed: 21 flags, and each player may remove 1, 2, 3 or 4. Whoever takes the last flag still wins. Do you want to move first, and if so what do you take?
Yes, move first, and take 1.

The most you may remove is 4, so the group size is 5 and the poison positions are the multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15, 20.

21 is not one of them, so the first player is safe. Take 1 and leave 20. After that, whatever she removes, you remove what completes 5, and she gets 15, then 10, then 5, then 0.

Watch the trap in the second move. Someone running on the memorised "leave a multiple of 4" also takes 1 here and looks right. Then from 19 they leave 16, and 16 is a winning position for her. The correct target was 15.
Two more named cases the exam asks about directly.
The ultimatum game. Player A proposes how to split a sum of money. Player B accepts (both get it) or rejects (both get nothing). Backward induction says player B should accept even one cent, so player A should offer almost nothing. In the lab that is not what happens. The most common offer, the modal offer, is 50%. The middle offer, the median, is about 40%. Offers below 10% are rare, and offers under 20% are often rejected in anger. In the dictator game, where player B cannot refuse, the median share is 25%. So the behaviour is part fairness and part a correct fear of being rejected.

The 1984 Orange Bowl. This was a big American football final. After scoring, a team may take a safe kick worth one point, or try a risky play worth two. Nebraska were behind and scored twice. Their coach took the safe extra point first and the risky two-point play second, and lost by one. He should have swapped the order. If the risky play fails early there is still a second chance. If it succeeds, the order never mattered. The lesson: if you must take a risk, take it as early as possible.

Formula sheet

The whole course on one page. A study aid only: the exam is closed book, so learn these by heart.

1. Sequential games (P2)

  • Backward induction: solve the last node first. Replace it with its payoff pair. Walk back to the start
  • Strategy = a plan for every node you could reach, not just one move
  • A tree that looks like a centipede is not always one. Only the payoffs decide

2. Prisoner's dilemma (P3)

  • R both cooperate · T you cheat alone · S you cooperate alone · P both defect. In a dilemma: T > R > P > S
  • Two-tick test: (1) both players have a dominant strategy, and (2) where they land is worse for both than another cell
  • Known last round: defect in every round (backward induction)
  • No end date: a payment of x every year forever is worth x / r today
  • Tipping point: r* = (R − P) / (T − R). Below r*: cooperate. Above: defect
  • Lab (Mengel 2018): waste (R − P) / R big → more cooperation · risk (P − S) / P big → less cooperation · temptation (T − R) / T small effect
  • About 45% cooperate in round 1, falling about 2% per round

3. Nash equilibrium (P4)

  • Underline: columns down for player 1 (first number), rows across for player 2 (second number). Two marks = pure Nash equilibrium
  • Test is stability, not quality: “would anyone move alone?”
  • “Pure” → zero is possible. “Pure or mixed” → every finite game has at least one
  • Strictly dominated = always worse. Weakly = never better, sometimes worse. Dominance-solvable → one unique equilibrium
  • Cournot: P = a − Q₁ − Q₂ · profit (P − c) × Q

4. Mixed strategies and zero-sum (P5)

  • First check: a dominant strategy? A saddle point? Neither → mix
  • Indifference: make the opponent's two expected payoffs equal. Solve for your p
  • Expected payoff of a row: q × (left) + (1 − q) × (right)
  • Chance of one cell: p × q, or p × (1 − q) when crossed
  • Value (player 1's payoff) = expected payoff of either of her rows
  • Zero-sum: one number per cell. Player 2 gets minus that

5. The 2/3-average game (P1, P4)

  • Average of a uniform range: (min + max) / 2
  • Mixed crowd: share₁ × avg₁ + share₂ × avg₂, then × 2/3 once, at the end
  • Nash equilibrium = 1

6. Credible strategies (P6)

  • Threat test: “would I really carry it out when the moment came?”
  • A move works only if made before the other side chooses, and visible to them
  • 8 devices: contracts · reputation · cutting off communication · burning bridges · leaving the outcome beyond your control · moving in small steps · teamwork · mandated negotiating agents

7. Lab numbers

  • Ultimatum: median offer about 40% (most common 50%; offers under 20% often rejected)
  • Dictator: median about 25% · Golden Balls: about 50% split
