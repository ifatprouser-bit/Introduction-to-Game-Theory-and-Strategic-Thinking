Exam map + backward induction

P1P2

What the exam actually is, and then the cheapest marks on it: game trees. Sequential games, where players move one after another, are about 11% of the paper. Every past paper has at least one.

⏸ Guess first

A game tree has five endings. The very best ending for player 1 pays her 9. That is more than any other ending in the tree. To reach it, player 1 moves down, and then player 2 must move up. Player 2 gets 1 if he moves up, and 8 if he moves down instead.

Will player 1 walk away with 9?

Take 20 seconds. Commit to an answer before you read on.

🎧 Why you solve a tree backwards ~1 min

A quick map of the exam, then the plan for the next half hour. Each section below has its own short audio.

  • Game tree: a picture of who moves when. Forks are decisions, branches are actions, the endings carry a payoff for each player.
  • Backward induction: solve the last decision first, then the one before it, and keep walking left. The professor's phrase: look forward and reason backward.
  • Strategy: not a move. A complete plan that says what you would do at every point you could ever be asked to choose.
  • The surprising one: more freedom of action can leave you worse off, because everyone else can see what you will do with the extra options.
  • Named cases to know: Fredo & Charlie, President vs Congress, the 21-flags game, the ultimatum game, the centipede game, the 1984 Orange Bowl.

One line to hold onto: a number in the tree is not a number you can get. Only the reachable endings are real.

Reading a tree, and solving it from the last node P2

🎧 Listen to this section

The phrase sequential game with perfect information is really two separate conditions, and you have to check both.

One: players move in turn, not at the same moment. Two: when it is your turn you can see everything that has already happened: every past move, and all the payoffs.

Haggling in a market meets both. I say a price out loud. You hear it, and you answer with your own price. But drop either condition and you have a different kind of game. If we each write our number on paper and show it at the same moment, the first condition fails. If I can see your last move but not the card in your hand, the second one fails. The game is still sequential, but it is no longer perfect information. Keeping these two apart is worth a mark on its own.

These games are drawn as trees. Read them left to right, like time. Each fork is a moment where one named player chooses. Each branch is one action. Each ending on the right carries a pair of numbers: player 1's payoff first, player 2's second.

Why you must work backwards: Fredo & Charlie

Presentation 2 opens with this. A businessman in a country with weak courts (Fredo) asks a foreign investor (Charlie) to put $100,000 into a business that will return $500,000. Fredo promises to split the profit after a year.

What happensCharlieFredo
Charlie doesn't invest00
Charlie invests, Fredo honours the deal150,000250,000
Charlie invests, Fredo keeps everything−100,000500,000

Charlie cannot answer "should I invest?" until he answers "what will Fredo do once the money is already here?" So settle Fredo's node first: keeping everything beats splitting. Now Charlie's real choice is between losing $100,000 and getting nothing. He stays home. The unique backward-induction outcome is (0, 0). Both men would have preferred the honest deal. That is what the lecture means by an inefficient equilibrium.

Solve the last node first, because at the last node there is nothing left to predict. Then replace that node with the payoff pair it produces, and repeat. Keep walking left until you reach the start.

A full tree, solved

Player 1 moves first, at the round node. Player 2 moves at the square nodes. Player 1's payoff is always written first.

The thick green line is the solved path. Player 1 plays B, then player 2 plays R, and the game ends at (3, 8). So player 1 gets 3. The grey dashed branches are what a player would choose at a node the game never reaches. They matter while you are solving, but nobody ever walks down them. Notice that no line reaches the circled (9, 1). It is the biggest payoff player 1 can see anywhere, but player 2 stands in the way, and he would rather have 8 than 1.

Worked example

In the tree above, what is the payoff of player 1 in the backward-induction equilibrium?
1
Start at the last node: the round node deep on branch B, where player 1 chooses again.
X ends at (9, 1); Y ends at (4, 5). Player 1 reads the first number.
9 > 4  →  she plays X. This node is worth (9, 1).
2
Move one step left: player 2's node on branch B.
L now leads to (9, 1); R ends at (3, 8). Player 2 reads the second number.
8 > 1  →  he plays R. This node is worth (3, 8).
3
Now the other side: player 2's node on branch A.
L ends at (6, 2); R ends at (2, 7).
7 > 2  →  he plays R. This node is worth (2, 7).
4
Finally the root. Both of player 1's branches now have a value.
A is worth (2, 7); B is worth (3, 8).
3 > 2  →  she plays B.
✓ The equilibrium outcome is (3, 8). Player 1 gets 3, player 2 gets 8.
Look at what you would have to choose from. An exam version of this question would offer 9, 6, 4, 3 and 2. Every one of those is a real payoff of player 1 printed in the tree. Four of them are unreachable. Recognising a number proves nothing.
Same tree, one change: the ending on branch B marked R is changed from (3, 8) to (3, 0). Nothing else moves.

Now what does player 1 get?
She gets 9. Redo it from the last node. The deep node is unchanged, so it is still worth (9, 1). Player 2 on branch B now compares 1 (from L) with 0 (from R). This time he prefers L. So branch B is worth (9, 1). At the root, B gives player 1 nine against A's two, so she plays B.

One number changed at one ending, and the whole answer moved from 3 to 9. That is why you never guess a tree from its shape.

The three traps in a tree question P2

🎧 Listen to this section

Trap 1: the wrong options are numbers from the tree. The professor builds the wrong options out of payoffs that are already printed in the picture. So no option ever looks obviously wrong. The most tempting one is the biggest number player 1 could ever get. If the player who moves before her would never send her down that branch, that number is only decoration.

Trap 2 is the big one, and it needs a picture. A centipede game is a long chain: at each step the player whose turn it is can stop and take a payoff, or continue and hand the decision on. The pot grows as you go, but whoever stops takes the bigger share. In the classic version, backward induction says stop immediately. That is the famous, sad result.

A tree that looks like a centipede is not always a centipede. The shape tells you nothing. Only the numbers at the endings decide.
Green again marks the branch each player chooses at his own node. Follow green from the left-hand node to find the outcome. The two trees have exactly the same shape and exactly the same numbers, except at the very last ending. There player 2's payoff is 20 in Version 1 and 12 in Version 2. That single number decides whether player 2 continues or stops at the last node. That decision then travels all the way back down the chain. Work out for yourself where each version ends up before you read on.

Worked example

Take Version 2 above. Show that player 1 stops immediately, and say what each player gets.
1
Last node: player 2, the fourth node.
Stop gives him 16. Continue gives him 12.
16 > 12  →  he stops. This node is worth (6, 16).
2
Third node: player 1.
Stop gives her 12. Continue leads to (6, 16), so it gives her 6.
12 > 6  →  she stops. This node is worth (12, 4).
3
Second node: player 2.
Stop gives him 8. Continue leads to (12, 4), so it gives him 4.
8 > 4  →  he stops. This node is worth (2, 8).
4
First node: player 1.
Stop gives her 4. Continue leads to (2, 8), so it gives her 2.
4 > 2  →  she stops. The game ends before it starts.
✓ Outcome (4, 0). Player 1 gets 4. She takes 4 and player 2 gets nothing. Both of them would prefer the ending worth 14 and 12 at the far right. That is the centipede paradox.

Now take Version 1, where only that one last number is different. Player 2 continues at the last node (20 beats 16), so player 1 continues at the third (14 beats 12), so player 2 continues at the second (20 beats 8), so player 1 continues at the first (14 beats 4). The game runs to the end at (14, 20), and player 1 gets 14. One number changed, and 4 became 14.
This really happened in the course's past papers. One paper has a tree of this shape, and its solution says it clearly: "Although the game looks superficially like a centipede game, it is not. P2 is better off continuing in the last node, which induces both players to continue in all previous nodes." There the right answer was the largest number on the option list. In 2024 B question 6, a real centipede, the answer was the smallest option, 11, out of 11 / 17 / 24 / 29 / none of the other answers. Same visual shape, opposite direction. You cannot guess from the option sizes either.

Trap 3: check which player is asked. This is the smallest trap and the most expensive one. You can solve the whole tree correctly and still hand in the wrong half of the pair. Which player they ask for changes from paper to paper. One paper asked for player 1 (=I) in a three-player tree. Another used the same three-player format and asked for player 2 (=II). A third called the players A and B instead of numbering them. The 2024 B paper called player 1 "the red player" and said his payoffs were the upper numbers, printed in red. The other player's payoff is always sitting in the option list, waiting for you. Solve first, then read the question again before you pick.

In a four-node chain like the ones above, you have solved the last three nodes and found that the second node (player 2's first turn) is worth (2, 8). At the first node, player 1 can stop and take (9, 1).

Do you need to look at anything else to answer "what does player 1 get"?
No. She gets 9, and you are done. Stopping gives her 9; continuing gives her whatever the second node is worth to her, which is 2. Nine beats two, so she stops.

This is the whole point of backward induction: once a node has been replaced by its value, everything to the right of it is finished business. You never need to look at those endings again. That is exactly why the big unreachable numbers out there cannot hurt you, unless you let them.

A strategy is a whole plan, and more freedom can hurt P1P2

🎧 Listen to this section

Presentation 1 gives the definition you must hold onto: a strategy is a rule determining a player's move in all possible cases. Not "what I will do", but "what I would do, everywhere I might be asked". Think of it as written instructions you hand to a stranger before leaving the room: he must be able to finish the game without phoning you.

This is why counting strategies gives bigger numbers than people expect. If player 2 moves at three different nodes and has two actions at each, he has 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 strategies. That holds even though he only ever makes one move in a single play of the game. Multiply across the nodes. Never add.

Move = one action at one node. Strategy = a complete instruction sheet covering every node you could reach, including the ones you expect never to visit.

The counter-intuitive result: President vs Congress

1987, in the United States. Two spending items are on the table. One is an antiballistic missile system (M), a defence project President Reagan wants. The other is urban renewal (U), money to rebuild poor city areas, which Congress wants. Both sides prefer having both items to having neither. Congress is the US parliament, and it decides what public money is spent on. So Congress chooses what to send. The President then signs the whole package or rejects all of it, which is called a veto. Payoffs run 4 (best) down to 1 (worst).

OutcomeCongressPresident
Both U & M pass33
U only41
M only14
Neither22

Once a year the President gives a speech to Congress called the State of the Union. In his 1987 speech, Reagan asked for a line-item veto: the power to cut single lines instead of rejecting the whole bill. That is strictly more freedom. Does it help him?

Worked example

Using the payoff table above, solve both versions of the game by backward induction: (a) the President can only approve or veto the whole package; (b) the President has a line-item veto and can delete single items. Compare the two outcomes.
1
Version (a): go to the President's node first, once for each thing Congress might send.
Vetoing anything produces "Neither", worth 2 to him.
Sent U+M: approve = 3 > 2  →  approve
Sent U: approve = 1 < 2  →  veto
Sent M: approve = 4 > 2  →  approve
2
Now Congress, knowing all of that. Read Congress's own column.
Send U+M → 3  |  Send U → 2  |  Send M → 1  |  Send nothing → 2
Best is 3. Congress sends the full package. Outcome: both items pass, (3, 3).
3
Version (b): the President's node again. Now he can keep any part of what arrives.
Sent U+M: he can take M only → 4. Best of {3, 4, 1, 2}  →  keep M only
Sent U: keep U = 1, delete it = 2  →  delete it
Sent M: keep M = 4  →  keep it
4
And Congress again.
Send U+M → 1  |  Send U → 2  |  Send M → 1  |  Send nothing → 2
Best is 2. Congress stops sending the package at all. Outcome: neither item passes, (2, 2).
✓ With the line-item veto the outcome falls from (3, 3) to (2, 2). That is Pareto-worse: worse for both players, including the President who gained the power. The lecture's insight: greater freedom of action can hurt a player.

Why? Because in a sequential game your options are also a promise. Once the President can cut the package apart, Congress knows he will, so Congress never offers it. Losing the ability to do something can be worth more than the ability itself.

Long games: the poison positions in the 21-flags game

Two players, 21 flags. On your turn you remove 1, 2 or 3 flags. Whoever takes the last flag wins. The tree is far too large to draw. But backward reasoning still works, because you only need to know which positions you lose from.

The red columns are the multiples of 4. If it is your turn and the count is a multiple of 4, you lose against a player who knows this. Otherwise, take enough to leave a multiple of 4, and keep doing it. Note that 21 is not a multiple of 4, so the player who moves first wins: take 1, leave 20.
You are playing the 21-flags game. It is your turn and 14 flags remain. How many do you take, and why?
Take 2, leaving 12. Twelve is a multiple of 4, so your opponent is now standing on a poison position. Whatever she removes (1, 2 or 3), you remove what completes 4 (3, 2 or 1), and you hand her 8, then 4, then 0. She never takes the last flag.

Check the other moves. Taking 1 leaves 13, and taking 3 leaves 11. Neither is a multiple of 4, so she can put you back on one.
Two more named cases the exam asks about directly.
The ultimatum game. A proposes how to split a sum of money. B accepts (both get it) or rejects (both get nothing). Backward induction says B should accept even one cent, so A should offer almost nothing. In the lab that is not what happens. The most common offer, the modal offer, is 50%. The middle offer, the median, is about 40%. Offers below 10% are rare, and offers under 20% are often rejected in anger. In the dictator game, where B cannot refuse, the median share is 25%. So the behaviour is part fairness and part a correct fear of being rejected.

The 1984 Orange Bowl. This was a big American football final. After scoring, a team may take a safe kick worth one point, or try a risky play worth two. Nebraska were behind and scored twice. Their coach took the safe extra point first and the risky two-point play second, and lost by one. He should have swapped the order. If the risky play fails early there is still a second chance. If it succeeds, the order never mattered. The lesson: if you must take a risk, take it as early as possible.
